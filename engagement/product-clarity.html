Engagement 01 · The product has become complicated

Product Clarity

Turn a complicated product into one agreed problem, a sequence of decisions, and a direction your team can judge.

Best for
A working product with competing priorities, or three accounts of what the problem is
Typical length
Two to four weeks, fixed scope and fixed fee, set after a first conversation
Your team’s involvement
One sponsor, four to seven short conversations, and one working session to decide
You leave with
An agreed problem, an ordered set of decisions, a tangible direction, and the reasoning in writing

Before you decide anything

Not sure this is the one? Start with a few sentences.

Tell me what is happening with your product, in your own words: what people keep asking for, which decision keeps getting put off, who disagrees about what. No deck, no call to book. I will read it and tell you plainly whether this engagement fits, and if it does not, what would.

If the number that comes back is more than you have, say so. There is usually a smaller version — two workflows rather than the whole product — and it is a different engagement rather than this one discounted.

Describe what is happening

The situation

The product works, and nobody can say plainly what it is for any more. Every release adds to it, and the roadmap is a list of requests rather than a position. Three people in the room have three accounts of the real problem, each partly right, which is why the conversation never resolves and the decision that matters keeps getting deferred: making it would mean telling somebody no.

This engagement is for that moment. Not a redesign, and not a discovery phase that ends in a deck: one senior person gets the problem stated in a sentence everyone recognizes, and puts something in front of you concrete enough to judge together. Clarity before commitment.

Three people in the room, three accounts of the real problem, each partly right.

Where it has worked

One project where naming the real problem was the turn. The figures are the case study’s own.

Midwest Couriers · 450+ trucks

Everyone assumed the answer was a map. Me included.

Each dispatcher manages fifty drivers at once. Sitting with them and watching them work turned the assumption over: they knew every location code by heart, and what slowed them down was scanning through endless lists. Naming that changed what got built, and the cards were tested with the people who would use them.

  • ~40%less time clearing the board
  • ~75%fewer wrong-vehicle dispatches

That reframing is what this engagement produces. There it went on to become the build; here it is the whole job, and you decide what follows.

How it works, step by step

Four steps, each one something you can check off. To show what each produces, they follow one company through, a composite rather than a client: a field-service business, eight years on one platform, three teams. Dispatch books the job, technicians close it on a phone, billing reconciles it after. The same job now exists in three places, and the roadmap holds forty requests and no position.

  1. Hear the problem from everyone who owns a piece of it

    Separately, and quickly: product, engineering, support, whoever fields the complaints. Where the accounts diverge is usually where the real problem is.

  2. State the problem in one sentence

    One sentence the whole room recognizes, including the people it constrains. Most of the value is here: a problem everyone can repeat back stops being reopened every other week.

  3. Sort what follows into an order

    What to decide next, what can wait, and what is a symptom of the first thing. A sequence, not a backlog. A backlog is what you already have.

  4. Make it tangible enough to judge

    Flows, a working prototype, whatever the decision actually needs. A visible direction makes a far better conversation than another description of one.

What it asks of you

  • One sponsor who can make the decision, or get it made
  • Four to seven people who own a piece of the problem, for about forty-five minutes each
  • The product and the roadmap as they actually are, not as presented
  • One working session, about half a day, where the decision gets made

How I work

You talk to the person doing the work: whoever hears the problem in the first week writes the recommendation in the last. A one-person practice runs few engagements at once, so the attention is undivided. There is a written update every week, and the reasoning is written down as the decisions get made rather than assembled on the last Friday — so the next hard call is one your team can make without turning it into archaeology.
