The situation

A product, a place or a small brand needs a visual identity or an illustration system, and the usual choices are a big agency that is too heavy, a logo marketplace that is too thin, or a pure illustrator: art without the product judgment about where and how the visuals will actually live. Meanwhile the thing itself works, but looks generic, and every new screen or page reinvents its own style.

This engagement is the missing middle: identity and illustration from someone who also understands software surfaces, so the language is designed for the places it will actually be used.

Identity work in practice: the Innovators Studio visual identity and the Learn to Live identity.

How it works, step by step

Four steps, in order, each one something you can check off. No step produces a deliverable for its own sake.

  1. Find the words before the pictures

    We agree what the brand should feel like before anything gets drawn: the words, the references, and the things it must never feel like. Explorations without this produce favorites, not decisions.

  2. Explore wide, choose narrow

    A handful of genuinely different directions, then one chosen for reasons we can both say out loud. The point of the wide pass is to make the final choice a comparison, not a leap of faith.

  3. Build it on the real surfaces

    The identity gets proven on your actual screens, pages and materials, not on posterboards. If a direction only works on a business card, it does not work.

  4. Hand over the language, not just the files

    The assets, plus the short guide that lets your team keep producing in the same language, including honest notes on where AI-assisted production genuinely helps and where it cheapens the work.

What you leave with

  • The identity itself: palette, type and the core marks, applied in place
  • An illustration approach your team can extend, reusable assets included
  • A short guide that keeps new work in the same language
  • Honest notes on where AI-assisted production helps, and where it does not

Working with one person

You talk to the person doing the work

No account layer, no team of juniors briefed second-hand, no rewriting of what you said before it reaches the person designing. You explain the problem once, to the person who will be solving it.

The attention is undivided and it is finite

A one-person practice takes on few engagements at a time, which is the point: yours gets the whole of the attention while it runs. It is also the constraint, and the honest reason to start a conversation earlier than you need to.

Where this is not the right fit

You want a logo by Friday at marketplace prices. This engagement is judgment about a whole visual language, applied where it lives, and it competes on that judgment rather than on speed.

Shape, scope and cost

Every engagement is scoped to the problem in front of it, so the length and the price come out of a first conversation rather than a menu. That conversation is free and usually short.
