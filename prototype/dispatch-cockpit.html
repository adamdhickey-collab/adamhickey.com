Working prototype · self-directed · synthetic data

A dispatch cockpit that shows its work

A load needs a truck, and recommending one is only half the problem. The dispatcher still has to see why that truck fits, what the system may be missing, and when to choose differently. So this prototype puts the reasons, the outcome record and the whole fleet beside the recommendation. The hypothesis: people spend less time reconstructing a recommendation when they can see what needs checking. Not acceptance without question. Better-directed attention.

A self-directed prototype on synthetic data. The interface works, and the ranking is computed on the page by a weighted rule over the factors it shows. The fleet, customer, drivers, loads and outcome records are invented; the towns are real. No model or dispatch system is connected.

Informed by the dispatch cockpit I led at RBA for Midwest Couriers, a fleet of more than 450 trucks, but a separate exploration rather than a reproduction, and never put in front of a dispatcher. Four situations, mouse or keyboard, about ninety seconds.

The cockpit

One load, seven trucks, four situations. Pick one; the rail beside the cockpit says what to look for and what to try. Assign the recommended truck, or any other that can legally take the load. Everything works from the keyboard: arrow keys move between situations, Tab reaches every control, and what changes is announced.

What to look for

    The dispatcher is not short on data

    A dispatcher at a fleet that size sees every truck, every driver’s hours, every load in the window and every exception, on three monitors, all day. Information is not what is scarce. Attention is. The assumption here: about four seconds to weigh five factors against seven trucks, forty times an hour, while the phone rings.

    Having the system recommend the truck is the obvious move, and it solves half the problem. A ranking that is right 95 times in 100 still gets checked 100 times if the dispatcher cannot tell which five to worry about.

    So the hypothesis is specific: dispatchers spend less time reconstructing a recommendation when its reasons, its limits and the shape of its past failures are easy to inspect. Not less checking; better-aimed checking. Whether explanations deliver that is unsettled in the research, which has seen them help where they cut the effort of checking and hurt where they added to it. An interface can pose the question well. It cannot answer it alone.

    The argument in prose is Enterprise AI should help people decide, not just answer. The cockpit above is the same argument as an interface.

    Four things the interface has to do

    Each is a position, and each is a specific thing on the screen above. Switch this page’s copy off and the four should still read from the cockpit alone.

    1. Show what went into the call, not just the call.

      A ranking is a summary of reasons. Hide them and the dispatcher has to reconstruct them to check the ranking, which is more work than ranking by hand. So the card lists what was weighed, with each value and where it stands against the rest of the fleet, and says what those comparisons are not: ahead of the fleet on a factor is not the same as that factor having decided it. No composite score, because a score is the one number that cannot be checked against anything.

      On screen: the factor list in the recommended truck’s card, and the same columns in the data table.

    2. Express confidence as a record of outcomes, not a number.

      “87% confident” is a claim about the system. “On time on 38 of the last 40 loads like this one” is a claim about loads, which a dispatcher can set beside the load in front of them. Read it for what it is: an on-time delivery does not prove the system picked the best truck, and a late one does not prove it picked the wrong one. So the two late loads are the useful part, each with what differed. If the misses have a shape, the dispatcher learns the shape, and that is when checking becomes selective instead of universal.

      On screen: the Outcomes on similar loads card, with the late deliveries behind a disclosure and what to watch for under each.

    3. Make the override cheap, and make it teach.

      The dispatcher has the phone call the system cannot hear. Overriding is one click on any truck that can take the load, with no confirmation dialog, because a dialog taxes the judgment that is the point. That is a claim about what Assign does here, where it stages a choice Undo reverses; where Assign sends a driver, a confirmation earns its place at that boundary. A hard requirement is different: a driver without the hours keeps their row and the reason, and has no Assign to press. After the override, and only after, an optional question asks why, lists the likely reasons, and says in plain words what happens to the answer. Skip submits nothing and changes nothing.

      On screen: Assign on every eligible row and the rule in place of it on the one that is not; the question above the data table after an override; Undo where Assign was.

    4. Keep the manual path whole.

      The recommendation speeds the decision and never gates it. The whole fleet stays on screen, every column sortable, never collapsed behind the suggestion. A truck the system could not rank keeps its row and says why. If the recommendation vanished tomorrow, this would still be a dispatch screen.

      On screen: the data table, in every situation and every state.

    What I would test next, and where I expect to be wrong

    This has not been in front of a dispatcher, and the first session would change it. The one question: does the interface help dispatchers decide well with less unnecessary checking, and without more missed constraints or more bad recommendations accepted? I would ask it as a comparison, the data table alone against the table with the recommendation, over matched scenarios: a straightforward call, a plausible wrong one, and a load with stale information. Read the quality of the decision, the time it took, the problems noticed, and the reason given. Opening a disclosure is worth recording; it is not evidence of judgment.

    Three smaller questions, and what each would do to the design.

    • Whether anyone opens the misses. The design leans on the two late loads being read. If nobody expands them, the record is a percentage with extra words, and the misses move up: into the recommendation line, or surfaced when the current load resembles one of them.
    • Whether the reasons list is the right list. Three reasons were guessed. The free-text answers in the first month are the real list, and one of them will be “the customer called,” which the system cannot see and should stop pretending to weigh.
    • Whether the tie state is honest or annoying. Declining to lead is right for a system that knows its own shape, and may be the thing dispatchers hate most at four on a Friday. If the tradeoff sentence is not enough to decide from in the seconds the work allows, the tie should show what the dispatcher did last time for this customer, and say so.

    Where I expect to be wrong: the factor list. Five was a choice for a readable card. Real dispatchers weigh things it does not name, the driver’s home time, the receiver’s dock, who is owed a good load, and the first thing they will say is that the list is missing the one that decides it. That is the right complaint, and the first sessions should be free to argue with the structure, not only fill in the card.
