A dispatch cockpit that shows its work

A truck recommendation is only useful when a dispatcher can judge it. This prototype puts the reasons, the outcome record and the whole fleet beside the recommendation, and leaves the call with the person doing the work.

Try the cockpit · about 90 seconds See the design decisions →

Self-directed, on invented data, and never tested with a dispatcher. The interface works. The ranking is computed on the page by a weighted rule over the factors it shows, not by a model, and no dispatch system is connected. The fleet, customer, drivers, loads and outcome records are invented; the towns are real.

Informed by the dispatch cockpit I led at RBA for Midwest Couriers, a fleet of more than 450 trucks, but a separate exploration rather than a reproduction.

Try the cockpit

One load, seven trucks, four situations. Pick one; the rail beside the cockpit says what to look for and what to try. Assign the recommended truck, or any other that can legally take the load. Everything works from the keyboard: arrow keys move between situations, Tab reaches every control, and what changes is announced.

What to look for

    The hard part is knowing what needs a second look

    A dispatcher at a fleet of 450 trucks has a load to place, seven trucks that could take it, and half the information arriving from outside the screen. Information is not what is scarce. Attention is.

    A ranking that is right 95 times in 100 still gets checked 100 times if the dispatcher cannot tell which five to worry about. So recommending the truck solves half the problem and leaves the expensive half standing.

    The design question Can the interface help someone check the right things without rebuilding the whole decision themselves?

    Assumptions behind the prototype

    These are assumptions, not observations. Nobody was timed and nothing here was measured: about four seconds to weigh five factors against seven trucks, forty times an hour, while the phone rings. They set the size of the card and the number of factors on it, and the first session with a dispatcher is as likely to move them as to confirm them.

    The hypothesis they support is specific: dispatchers spend less time reconstructing a recommendation when its reasons, its limits and the shape of its past failures are easy to inspect. Not less checking; better-aimed checking. Whether explanations deliver that is unsettled in the research, which has seen them help where they cut the effort of checking and hurt where they added to it. An interface can pose the question well. It cannot answer it alone.

    The argument in prose is Enterprise AI should help people decide, not just answer. The cockpit above is the same argument as an interface.

    Four decisions, and what each one looks like

    Each is a position, and each is a specific thing on the screen above. Switch this page’s copy off and the four should still read from the cockpit alone.

    1. Show why this truck fits

      The selection factors sit beside the recommendation, each with its value and where it stands against the rest of the fleet available for this load. The dispatcher checks the advice against the trucks instead of reconstructing the reasoning from scratch.

      Why I designed it this way

      A ranking is a summary of reasons. Hide them and the dispatcher has to rebuild them to check the ranking, which is more work than ranking by hand.

      The card is careful about what the comparison is not. Ahead of the fleet on a factor is not the same as that factor having decided the call, and the note under the list says so. There is no composite score, because a score is the one number that cannot be checked against anything: it hides the five figures a dispatcher could actually argue with behind one they cannot.

    2. Make the exceptions easy to inspect

      A record of on-time deliveries is useful context. The exceptions are more useful: they say what went differently, and what might deserve a look on this load. So the misses open in place, each with what to watch for.

      Why I designed it this way

      “87% confident” is a claim about the system. “On time on 38 of the last 40 loads like this one” is a claim about loads, which a dispatcher can set beside the load in front of them.

      Read either for what it is: an on-time delivery does not prove the system picked the best truck, and a late one does not prove it picked the wrong one. That is why the two late loads carry what differed rather than a tally. If the misses have a shape, the dispatcher learns the shape, and that is the moment checking becomes selective instead of universal.

    3. Make changing the choice easy

      The dispatcher has the phone call the system cannot hear. Choosing another truck is one press on any row the rule allows, with no dialog in the way. The question about why comes after the assignment, and Skip weighs the same as Save.

      Why I designed it this way

      A confirmation dialog taxes the judgment that is the point. That is a claim about what Assign does here, where it stages a choice Undo reverses; where Assign sends a driver, a confirmation earns its place at that boundary.

      A hard requirement is a different thing from a low score. A driver without the hours keeps their row and the reason, and has no Assign to press. And the prompt says in plain words what happens to the answer, because a prompt that does not say is a prompt people learn to skip.

    4. Keep the whole fleet in reach

      The recommendation is assistance, not the only route through the task. Every column sorts, every eligible row assigns, and nothing is collapsed behind the suggestion. If the recommendation vanished tomorrow, this would still be a dispatch screen.

      Why I designed it this way

      A truck the system could not rank keeps its row and says why. T-114 below is nine miles from the pickup and wins on almost everything; its driver is short on hours, so it cannot legally take the load. That is a rule, not a bad score, and it reads as one: the row stays, the shortfall is written next to the hours, and the button says it cannot be pressed rather than disappearing.

      The table is also the reason this page shows the capture at the width it does. The fleet is 1,200 pixels of interface because the argument it makes is that seven trucks and five factors stay in reach at once; shrunk into a column beside a paragraph, the page would be claiming the table is legible while showing that it is not.

    What I need to learn from dispatchers

    The prototype demonstrates an interaction approach. It does not establish that the approach improves decisions. This has not been in front of a dispatcher, and the first session would change it.

    Over matched scenarios: a straightforward call, a plausible wrong one, and a load where the information on screen is stale. The one question is whether the interface helps dispatchers decide well with less unnecessary checking, and without more missed constraints or more bad recommendations accepted. I would read the quality of the decision, the time it took, the problems noticed and the reason given. Opening a disclosure is worth recording; it is not evidence of judgment.

    1. Are decisions better, not just faster?

      Decision quality first, then missed constraints, checking effort and time. A faster wrong answer is the failure mode this design could plausibly cause, so it is the one to look for.

    2. Does anyone open the exceptions?

      The design leans on the two late loads being read, and on whether they change what someone does — not on whether the disclosure was clicked. If nobody opens them, the record is a percentage with extra words, and the misses move up: into the recommendation line, or surfaced when the current load resembles one of them.

    3. Does a close call help, or annoy?

      Declining to lead is right for a system that knows its own shape, and may be the thing dispatchers hate most at four on a Friday. If the tradeoff sentence is not enough to decide from in the seconds the work allows, the tie should show what the dispatcher did last time for this customer, and say so.

    My biggest assumption

    The five factors on the card may not be the factors that decide the real job. Real dispatchers weigh things the list does not name — the driver’s home time, the receiver’s dock, who is owed a good load — and the first thing they will say is that the one that decides it is missing. That is the right complaint. The first sessions should be free to argue with the structure, not only to fill in the card.

    The three reasons in the override prompt were guessed the same way. The free-text answers in the first month are the real list, and one of them will be “the customer called,” which the system cannot see and should stop pretending to weigh.
