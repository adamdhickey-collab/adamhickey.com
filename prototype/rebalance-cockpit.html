A rebalancing call on live bike-share data that a person can check and overrule

A Citi Bike station has run out of bikes. The system picks where a van should fetch more from. The person decides whether it is right.

  • Every station and count is real, read from the public feed.
  • Old counts are marked with their age.
  • Close calls are flagged, and a rule beats a score.
  • Sending the van somewhere else takes one press.

What changed when the data got real

The dispatch cockpit runs on a fleet I made up, and I wrote its hardest moments on purpose: a stale hours figure, a tie, a driver over the legal limit. I wanted to know which of those survive when nobody writes the data.

Most of the screen carried over untouched. Five things changed, and each one taught me something about the original.

  1. The load became a station, and the fleet became its neighbors

    A truck taking a load and a van fetching bikes are the same decision. One place needs something, several could supply it, and each has reasons for and against.

    The empty station sits where the load did. The stations nearest it sit where the trucks did, ranked on distance, bikes to spare and how full they are.

  2. The stale mark had less to do than I expected

    In the synthetic version I invented an old figure to show the mark. The feed says when every station last checked in, so I expected old counts everywhere.

    On the reading saved with this page, 24 of about 2,500 stations had gone quiet for half an hour, and all 24 had stopped renting. The rule removes them before their age matters. The mark stays for the one that doesn’t.

  3. There is no record, so the card says what the page has seen

    The originalThe original’s strongest card was a record: 38 of 40 on time, and what went wrong on the other two. A public feed only knows the present.rsquo;s strongest card was a record, invented like the rest of its data: 38 of 40 on time, and what went wrong on the other two. A public feed only knows the present.

    I could have invented a history to fill the card. Instead it counts the readings this page has seen since you opened it, and says what it can’t know.

  4. A new reading waits for a press

    A new reading arrives every minute. Applied as it lands, it could reorder the ranking under someone halfway through checking it.

    So it waits behind a button that says what time it is from. The only thing that ticks on its own is the age of the reading on screen.

  5. My assumptions are labeled as mine

    The feed doesn’t say how many bikes a station should keep for its own riders, how full an empty one should be refilled, or how many a van holds.

    The rule rests on the first of those, so the screen calls it my assumption in the sentence that applies it. Nobody should mistake it for the operator’s.

What a real operator has and this page doesn’t is where the vans are and where riders will be in an hour. With those, the screen would rank vans as well as stations. The parts that matter would stay: the reasons, the age of every figure, the rule that can’t be argued with, and a person who can say no in one press.
