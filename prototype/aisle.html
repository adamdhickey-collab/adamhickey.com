A shopping trip that keeps its context, from a photo to the aisle

You photograph a lamp at a friend’s. The phone finds four like it and says why. Two days later you are standing in the aisle, and what you learned at home is still with you.

Try the prototype Read the concept behind it →

Problem
What a person learns while shopping at home does not come into the store with them, so the trip starts over at the door.
Hypothesis
Carry the photo, the shortlist, and the comparison across, say how sure the app is about the shelf, and hand off to a person on purpose, and the trip reads as one experience.
My role
Concept, service design, interaction, and visual design, and the front-end build, with Claude Code.
Artifact
A working browser prototype at a phone’s width. No image search, no inventory system and no model behind it: every lamp, count, and person is written at the top of aisle.js.
Context
An independent concept. Not client work, and not affiliated with or sponsored by any retailer. The store, Marigold Home, is made up. The write-up has the journey, the blueprint, and what I would test first.
Next
Put it in front of five people who shopped for something they saw somewhere else, half of them in a store, and find out whether the carried context is used or ignored.

Not tested with shoppers or with store staff. The matches are written, not computed: “Find lamps like this” shows the same four every time.

Try the two-minute prototype

One trip, seven screens. Three of them are at home and four are in the store, and the line over the phone says which half you are in. Everything you can press does something, and nothing you press is final.

Three things to try

  1. Open “Why this one” on a match, then hide one with “Not it” and bring it back.
  2. Save two, plan the trip, and find the shelf count the app will not vouch for.
  3. In the store, compare them, say one is missing, and see what the app offers before it offers a person.

What this is

  • Designed and built by me: plain HTML, CSS, and JavaScript, no framework, no build step.
  • Keyboard accessible, with every change announced; it holds at a phone’s width and sits in a phone on a wider screen.
  • Sample data and a written story, not a model. The lamps, the store, the counts, and Dana are all in aisle.js.

Five decisions, and what each one looks like

  1. Carry the context, not the cart

    What travels into the store is the photo, the shortlist, and what was compared, because that is what the person was thinking with at home. A cart carries what they decided; this carries how far they got.

    Why I designed it this way
    • The photo is the question. Everything after it is an answer to “is this the one?”, so it stays on screen where the comparison can be checked against it.
    • A shortlist is a decision in progress. Two lamps saved means the choice was not made at home, and the store is where it gets made. The app’s job is to keep both in reach.
    • The store screen opens on the list, not on a map. A map answers “where” and the aisle number does that in four characters. What the person wants is the thing they were deciding about.

  2. Say why, and how sure

    Every match carries a level in plain words, one line saying what matched and what did not, and the reasons behind a disclosure. A photo cannot tell a lamp’s height, so the list says that where it matters instead of printing a number it does not have.

    Why I designed it this way
    • A percentage is a claim about the system. “Same shade, different legs” is a claim about the lamp, which a person can check against the photo. This is Agent Review’s rule and Dispatch Cockpit’s: the evidence beside the answer, in terms the person can argue with.
    • The weak match stays on the list and says it is weak. “A stretch” teaches the shape of what the app can and cannot see, which is what lets a person decide when to trust it.
    • The uncertainty is a sentence, not a color. The close match takes the sage and the stretch the gray, and the words carry it on a screen with neither.

  3. Make no cheap

    “Not it” sits beside “Save” on every match, costs one press, and can be undone from the line that reports it. Rejecting a suggestion is how a person tells the app what they meant, and an app that makes that hard is choosing not to learn.

    Why I designed it this way
    • A no that costs more than a yes is not a choice. The two chips are the same size and the same press, so the list never leans on the person to accept.
    • Undo, not a warning. The hidden lamp is named in a status line with the way back on it, so a slip costs a press and no dialog interrupts the one thing the person is doing.
    • Hiding all four is allowed. The empty list says so and offers them back, because “none of these” is a real answer and the app should hear it.

  4. Treat a shelf count as a claim with an age

    Three on the shelf, counted this morning, is one kind of fact. One on the shelf, counted two days ago, is a guess, and it says so in the caution ink before the trip, so that nobody drives four miles on a number the store cannot stand behind.

    Why I designed it this way
    • Operational data has an age, and a system that knows its evidence is thin should say so rather than round it up to yes. The cockpit marks a stale hours reading the same way.
    • The age is printed beside the count, every time, so a reader learns to look for it rather than being warned once.
    • When the shelf is empty the app does not argue. “The shelf is the truth” is the first line of the next screen, and the four ways forward each say how they differ from what the person came for.

  5. Hand off to a person on purpose

    “Ask someone in Home” is on the store screen from the start and again when a lamp is missing. When Dana comes, the screen says exactly what she can see: the photo, the shortlist, the comparison, and the empty shelf, and nothing else.

    Why I designed it this way
    • A person is the right tool for some of this. A back room, a near match that needs an eye, a shopper who is not sure what they are asking: these settle faster in conversation than on a screen, and the app should know which moment it is in.
    • The handoff carries the context too. Otherwise the person starts the trip over a third time, at the counter.
    • What the employee sees is stated, and it is less than the app knows. Not the name, not the account, not the history. The line between helpful and intrusive is drawn in words the shopper can read.
    • Walking it back is one press. “Keep browsing instead” sits next to “Done”, because asking for help should not commit anyone to waiting.

What I need to learn from shoppers and from staff

The prototype shows a transition. It does not show that people use what it carries, or that an employee wants to be handed a stranger’s shortlist. The first session with either would change it, and the write-up says which assumptions to test first and what result would prove the idea wrong.

My biggest assumption

That a person in an aisle wants to look at their phone at all. If the shelf answers the question faster than the comparison does, the store half of this app should shrink to the aisle number and the handoff, and the work was in getting them to the right aisle with the right two lamps in mind.

The hard part of a cross-channel experience is not the channels. It is the context: what a person learned, what they are still deciding, and how sure anyone is about the shelf. Carry that across, say how sure you are, and know when to stop and find a person.
