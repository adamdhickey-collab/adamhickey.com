When does a product need a design system?

When more than one team is building the same component differently, and the product shows it. Not when the pattern library is missing: when the fourth version of the same data table ships. Before that point a shared file and a conversation are enough. After it, every new screen restarts a decision that was settled somewhere else last year, and the AI tools the teams build with see four data tables and generate a fifth.

The sign is not the missing library

Most products that need a design system already have one, on paper. The folder of patterns exists. Nobody opens it, because it was never built against the products the teams actually ship.

So the question is not whether you have a design system. It is whether the people building the product agree on what a data table is.

Ask three teams. If you get three answers, and each one is defensible, that is the sign. A product suite that grew a product at a time shows it: four teams have built the same data table four ways, nobody is sure which one is correct, and every new screen restarts a conversation that was settled somewhere else last year.

The people using the product feel it before anyone building it does. In the enterprise suite I led UX for, someone in the field might open four of the apps before lunch and relearn the layout every time.

What it costs before you have one

That suite ran more than forty apps, and each one had been built by a different team at a different time. Design got pulled in at the end to clean things up, if it got pulled in at all. And no one could say whether any of it was working, because no one was measuring it.

None of that is a component problem. It is the cost of forty teams each solving the same problem reasonably, alone.

The cost lands in three places. Designers and developers guess, and rebuild things that exist. People who use the product relearn it screen by screen. And decisions that should be settled once get made again, by whoever is in the room, every time a screen ships.

What it costs after

A system is not free either, and the teams that resent one are usually right about why.

Not everything belongs in a system. Over-sharing produces a foundation every team resists, and the components teams resist are the ones they fork. The data table goes shared. The scheduling board one team argued for probably stays local, and the team that argued for it had good reasons.

A system without governance is a folder of components that slowly diverges again. Who can change what, how a team proposes an addition, how a change ships without breaking four products, and which numbers will say whether it is working: those rules are the system. The components are what the rules produce.

Governance nobody can reach is how the forking restarts. A standing half hour for what the system is missing does more than a review board that meets quarterly.

The third reader

For years a certain amount of ambiguity was survivable, because humans fill gaps. A designer who sees three nearly identical cards asks someone which one to use.

The AI tools teams now build with can ask a question, but not of the person who settled this one and never wrote it down. They see four data tables and generate a fifth. And once anyone on a team can ask an agent for a new component in minutes, the cost of divergence approaches zero. You no longer need neglect to create inconsistency. You only need every team to solve its immediate problem.

That makes the case for a system stronger at exactly the moment some people expect AI to make it weaker. Creation got cheap. Consistency did not.

What changed once the system was in place

In that suite, the shared library replaced hundreds of one-off components with a small reusable set, and cut the average time from design to development by about a third. Standards and accessibility checks written into the release process cut UI defects in production by more than half, quarter over quarter. Clearer flows cut new-user training time in half.

Those are the numbers a system earns when it is used. The number that says it was built is the component count, and nobody should be paid on that one.

So, when?

When a second team ships the same component a second way. When nobody can say which version is right. When design is invited at the end to tidy up, and nothing measures whether it helped.

If that is where you are, the first move is unglamorous and it is the whole basis of the work: inventory what already exists. Every component, in every product, including the four versions of the same one, and the tokens, variables and documentation behind them, gaps included. You cannot decide what should be shared until you can see what has been built twice.

If this is the situation you are in: Design System Foundation, a three-week diagnostic, then a build scoped from it. The account this draws on: Making 40+ enterprise apps feel like one product.
