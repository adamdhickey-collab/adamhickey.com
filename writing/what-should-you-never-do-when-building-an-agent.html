Lesson 5 of 12 · 3 min read

What should you never do when building an agent?

Never trust a success the agent reports about itself. A call that returns “OK” may have done nothing, a confident answer may be wrong, and an action nobody can stop will run when it shouldn’t. Many of the rules that prevent this are design decisions.

Three terms first

Tool call
An agent asking another system to do something: send, save, charge, or look up.
Irreversible action
One a person can’t take back with a button: money moved, a message sent, a record deleted.
Kill switch
A way for a person to stop an agent mid-run, and know what it had already done.

A status can be accurate and still not tell you enough

In After Send, my bill-paying concept on simulated data, a supplier calls a bakery it invoiced. The supplier’s records say unpaid. The bakery’s screen says Paid.

Both are right. The bakery sent a one-time card, and the supplier hasn’t charged it yet. “Paid” covers three steps, and the screen collapsed them into one word.

Every rule on the list cost someone an incident

Alistair Greenwood, who builds AI agents, listed 21 things he never does when building agents, each learned from something that broke in live use. One is After Send’s problem: a successful response is not proof that anything happened.

I sort his 21 into four questions a designer can answer:

The interface is where these rules become visible

Each rule needs a screen that keeps it. In After Send, the payment record shows when it last checked.

A confidence score only says how sure the system is. Where a fact came from, and how old it is, tells a person what to do.

A run doesn’t only succeed or fail. It can finish some steps, or send a request and hear nothing back, and each ending needs a different next move.

Unknown is the one that hurts. Retrying a payment that may have gone through is how someone pays twice, which is why After Send checks for a late charge first.

Stopping needs the same care: a kill switch nobody can read is just a button. A stop has to work mid-run, from the keyboard, and say what it left half-finished.

Not all 21 are mine to build. Engineers own the retry logic and the tests a release must pass. What counts as passing is a shared call, and I’d want to be in the room.

If the agent can say “done” when nothing happened, the screen has to say what actually did.

Check yourself

1Which status in your product could be accurate and still mislead someone?

Look for one word covering several steps. Each step that can be open on its own deserves its own line.

2If your agent had to be stopped right now, what would the person see?

If the answer is a spinner that goes away, the stop is designed for the system, not for the person who pressed it.
