Enterprise AI should help people decide, not just answer

A useful AI feature helps someone judge a recommendation and act on it well. The measure of success is the quality of the decision, and how often people accept the answer says little about that.

In this article

  1. A suspected cause is a place to start, not permission to act
  2. Finding, summarizing, suggesting, drafting and acting are five different products
  3. Put the evidence beside the recommendation
  4. Give people control that does something
  5. Prototype the wrong answer

Key idea 1 of 5

A suspected cause is a place to start, not permission to act

Picture a feature that groups failed orders and suggests a likely cause. It notices a batch of rejected orders that seem to share a missing customer identifier, and points at the rules that translate the partner’s documents.

That’s a good place to start an investigation, and not yet permission to change the rule or resubmit the orders. The design work lives in that distance.

I’ve built one working version of that distance, for a different job. In Dispatch Cockpit, a prototype, the system recommends a truck and the dispatcher decides whether it’s right.

It runs on synthetic data, ranks with a transparent weighted rule rather than a model, and hasn’t been in front of a working dispatcher. I use it because it’s the one I can show rather than describe.

Key idea 2 of 5

Finding, summarizing, suggesting, drafting and acting are five different products

Before choosing between a chat and a recommendation card, the team has to settle what job it’s giving the system. Each is a different product, with a different cost when it’s wrong.

Dispatch Cockpit is deliberately the third of the five. It suggests, and nothing on its screen acts without a press.

Crossing into acting is a conversation about reliability, permissions and recovery, held with product and engineering. A designer shouldn’t imply an action is safe because it was easy to draw as a button.

Part of settling the role is asking whether the task needs AI at all. In Dispatch Cockpit, the driver’s hours-of-service limit is a federal rule, enforced before any score is figured. There’s nothing about it to rank.

Key idea 3 of 5

Put the evidence beside the recommendation

Dispatch Cockpit puts five factors under the recommended truck, each with its value, its verdict and where it stands against the fleet. A score would have been simpler to draw. Five figures can be argued with.

The evidence has to come from the records rather than be generated beside the answer, because an explanation that sounds right and cites nothing is worse than no explanation.

When two trucks are level, the screen says so, names the tradeoff, and declines to pick. When one rests on a stale figure, the card says how old it is and what to check before assigning.

Design psychology

More trust is not the goal. Calibrated trust is.

The aim is reliance where it’s earned and questions where something is missing. A confidence figure is where this usually goes wrong: before putting one on a screen, say what it measures and watch a real user read it.

On explainability and trust: Google PAIR, Explainability + Trust

The design work lives in that distance.

Key idea 4 of 5

Give people control that does something

The dispatcher has the phone call the system can’t hear, so disagreeing has to be cheap. In Dispatch Cockpit, Assign sits on every row the rule allows, with no dialog in the way, and Skip weighs the same as Save.

Feedback needs the same honesty. If dismissing a suggestion only hides it, the interface shouldn’t imply the system just learned something.

The override prompt says where its answer goes: three matching reasons on one customer in a month send a review to the dispatch manager. Whether three is right is a guess, and the prototype’s page says so.

Every override should also leave a record someone who wasn’t there can read. Dispatch Cockpit saves the reason with the load. It doesn’t yet write down what it recommended, on which figures, so a production version would need both halves.

A more autonomous feature needs more than this: a scope and stopping conditions that engineering enforces. That’s a separate article.

Key idea 5 of 5

Prototype the wrong answer

Dispatch Cockpit was built around four scenarios rather than one happy path:

  • A clear pick
  • A close call where it declines to choose
  • A stale figure under one of the two level trucks
  • A rule that removes the truck that would otherwise rank first

I think the wrong answers are the ones worth building, because a correct suggestion teaches nobody where to look. What the prototype doesn’t have yet is the watching, or a case that changes while someone reviews it.

Simulated output is enough to test the interaction. But a successful prototype test says nothing about whether the model is accurate, and Dispatch Cockpit has no model at all.

Then measure the decision, not the acceptance. A high acceptance rate can mean the suggestions are good, or that people are clicking through them, so the study ranks decision quality first and time last.

In one breath

An answer is useful when the person can judge it and act on it: evidence beside the recommendation, controls that do something, and a record someone else can read.

If you do one thing: prototype the wrong answer before the right one, and watch whether anyone notices.
