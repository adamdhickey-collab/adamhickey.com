Why does designing with AI feel slower than it should?

Because most of the time goes to explaining, re-checking, and repairing, and those trace to three gaps: the tool doesn’t have the context, nobody decided what to hand it, and the team can’t yet tell a confident answer from a correct one. Each gap has a fix, and the tool vendors have already written most of them down.

Three terms first

Context
Everything the tool needs to know before it starts: research, rules, and what counts as done.
Delegation
Deciding which parts the tool builds and where a person checks before it goes on.
AI literacy
Knowing when a tool assumed something, and checking the result instead of the message saying it worked.

The same ten frustrations keep coming back

Devansh Tank collected them: explaining takes longer than designing, small edits break approved work, the output drifts from the design system, and prototypes pass for finished products.

I’ve had most of the ten. His point is that they aren’t ten problems. They’re three.

Context is the gap I hit most, and the cheapest to close

When I built After Send, I decided what the person on the call needed to see first, then used AI to build the first working version in a day. The day was possible because the decision came first.

The explanation is long because it was never written down.

Drift is the same gap. This site’s specs drifted from its stylesheet three times before a check read them against each other. Now the rules are where a tool reads them.

Delegation means choosing where you’ll look, before it starts

Tank’s advice is to stay in the work, not wait for a finished prototype: check the proposed steps, inspect one representative component, and test the error handling before calling a flow done.

In Agent Review’s simulated run, the job was to change nothing anyone could see. Writing that limit into the job is why the agent stopped on the one change that broke it.

Literacy is the slowest of the three. A completion message is a claim, and a team learns to check it the way it learned to check a pull request.

Most of the slowness is the work you skipped before you asked.

Check yourself

1What did you last explain to an AI tool that is written down nowhere else?

That’s context living in a chat. Put it where the next session, and the next person, will read it first.

2At what point do you check the tool’s work: during, or only at the end?

Checking only at the end is where approved work gets broken. One checkpoint on the plan saves most of the repair.
