How should you test a product now that AI can help?

Start from the decision the test has to settle, let agents clear the broken paths, then watch five people who do the work try real tasks, and measure each one. AI makes the work around a session cheaper. It can’t sit in the session for you.

In this article

  1. Start from the decision the test has to settle
  2. Let agents clear the broken paths before anyone sits down
  3. Test with five people who do the work, on the most real thing you can build
  4. Give people a goal, then watch and keep quiet
  5. Turn what you saw into a decision within two days

Key idea 1 of 5

Start from the decision the test has to settle

On the dispatch floor at Midwest Couriers, I was sure the new tool needed a map. A morning of watching the dispatchers work ended that: they knew every location code by heart.

I was testing my own idea that morning, and I didn’t know it. So now every test starts from a decision. Which layout goes forward? Can someone new assign a rush order without help?

The plan fits on a page: the decision, who I need, five or six tasks, and what counts as success on each. If no result would change the plan, I don’t run the test.

Key idea 2 of 5

Let agents clear the broken paths before anyone sits down

An hour with a real person is too expensive to spend on a dead link. Before anyone sees the build, agents walk every path and flag dead ends, empty states, contrast, and claims the screen can’t back up.

They’re good at it. An AI reader playing an engineering lead checked my Agent Review case study against its record and found claims I couldn’t support. I’d read that page a dozen times.

It can’t give me results. Nielsen Norman Group compared synthetic users with real studies (opens in a new tab). One said, “Yes, I completed all the courses I mentioned.” The real participant had finished three of seven.

Key idea 3 of 5

Test with five people who do the work, on the most real thing you can build

Five people a round finds most of what matters, if they’re the right five: people who do the work, and nobody who helped build it.

Two kinds of user, a dispatcher and a manager say, means five of each. Their problems rarely overlap.

I used to test clickable mockups. Now I can build a working prototype on real data in days, so people meet real states, real delays, and real mistakes.

The place matters as much. Lucy Learns passed every review and came apart in its first real session.

AI can shrink the work around a session. It can’t sit in it for you.

Key idea 4 of 5

Give people a goal, then watch and keep quiet

A task names the goal and never the button. At Midwest Couriers the question was “who is free right now?”, and dispatchers answered it from the status colors without scrolling.

In the session I ask people to think aloud, and I don’t help. When someone asks whether to press something, I ask what they’d expect it to do.

Each task gets three measures: did they finish, how long it took, and how hard it felt, on a one-to-seven rating they give right after. Then one question: what did you expect to happen?

I still watch every recording myself. A summary keeps what people said. It drops the long pause before they said it, and that pause is usually the finding.

Key idea 5 of 5

Turn what you saw into a decision within two days

Findings go stale fast, so I synthesize inside two days. An AI pass groups the transcripts into themes first, and I check every theme against the clips before it goes in.

Each problem gets a severity and a count: how badly it blocked the task, and how many of the five hit it. Three of five failing is a fix this sprint. One of five hesitating is a note.

The readout is short: the decision, what we saw, a few clips, what changes. The next round tests the fix.

After launch, numbers take over the how-often. On the SAP apps, feedback captured at the moment of use said where people were stuck. Sessions said why.

Where this runs out is my own lab. Agent Review isn’t tested with users yet, and Dispatch Cockpit hasn’t been in front of a working dispatcher. Both pages say what I believe, not what I’ve watched.

Agents have made those pages more honest. Only people can tell me whether they’re right.

An agent can tell you the door is locked. Only a person can tell you they didn’t want to go in.

In one breath

Name the decision first. Let agents clear the broken paths and rehearse the script, then watch five people who do the work try real goals on something real, measure each task, and decide within two days. Numbers say how often; people say why.

If you do one thing: rewrite one task in your next test so it names the goal and never the button.
