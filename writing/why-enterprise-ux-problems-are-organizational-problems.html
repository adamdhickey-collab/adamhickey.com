Why enterprise UX problems are usually organizational problems

When enterprise software is hard to use, the interface is where the problem showed up, not where it started. Behind an inconsistent product there are teams making decisions independently. Behind a stalled workflow there is a decision nobody is placed to make. Behind a design system nobody uses there is no rule for who settles what is shared. Fix the screens without fixing that, and you get cleaner screens with the same problem underneath.

Three accounts of the real problem

The product works, and nobody can say plainly what it is for any more. Every release adds to it, and the roadmap is a list of requests rather than a position. Three people in the room have three accounts of the real problem, each partly right, which is why the conversation never resolves.

And the decision that matters keeps getting deferred, because making it would mean telling somebody no.

Nothing about that is a UX problem in the sense that a designer can open a file and fix it. It is a problem of who decides, and it shows up in the interface because the interface is where every unmade decision eventually lands.

The interface is messy, and underneath it

Most first conversations about a difficult product start with the screens. They are inconsistent, or confusing, or people tolerate them rather than understand them.

Thirty minutes in, the more useful thing to say is usually: I think you may actually have two problems here. The interface is messy, but underneath that, three teams are making product decisions independently.

Knowing which is which is most of the job. Knowing when the interface is the problem and when the organization behind the interface is the problem. Knowing what needs to become consistent and what should remain flexible. Knowing when the team needs more options and when it needs someone to finally eliminate some.

What thirteen years of consulting taught me

For thirteen years I built the digital front doors for big companies, turning a tangle of internal complexity into something the person on the other side of the screen could actually use.

Consulting is a high-wire act. You walk into a room where everyone speaks a different language. Marketing wants flash, IT wants security, and the people using the thing just want to get their work done. My job was to be the translator.

That is where I learned that most design problems are communication problems, and that coherence at scale takes systems: design systems, governance, shared patterns, honest measurement. Not because the systems are the point, but because they are how an organization stops relitigating the same decision on every screen.

Three shapes it takes

Inconsistency

A company ran more than forty enterprise apps, each built by a different team at a different time. Someone in the field opened four of them before lunch and relearned the layout every time. Design got pulled in at the end to clean things up, if it got pulled in at all, and nobody could say whether any of it was working because nobody was measuring it.

The fix was a shared foundation, and the part that made it hold was organizational: checks written into the release process, teams trained to own the standard, and numbers that said whether it was working. The library was the visible half.

Paperwork

A federal grant network ran on old software, spreadsheets and workarounds, and no two of them agreed. Grantees spent more time chasing paperwork than running their programs, and the administrators helping them spent their days on the same paperwork, with no way to tell which programs were working.

The interviews told us what was stressful. The data told us what was slow. The two rarely pointed at the same thing, which is itself the finding: the tool was where the friction showed, and the friction came from a network with no single place anyone trusted. One portal replaced all of it, and processing a data request fell from forty-five to fifty-five hours to just over ten.

The wrong assumption, shared by everyone

A courier company with more than 450 vehicles asked for one real-time view of the fleet. Everyone assumed the answer was a map. Me included. Sitting with the dispatchers turned that over: they knew every location code by heart, and what slowed them down was scanning through endless lists. The right screen was dense, scannable cards.

The organizational problem there was smaller and just as real. The people specifying the tool and the people using it had never watched each other work. Board-clearing time fell by roughly thirty percent once they had.

The initiative that is funded and moving sideways

There is a fourth shape, and it is the quietest. An initiative that matters, that is funded and staffed with engineers, moving sideways because nobody senior owns the design of it and it is waiting on decisions nobody is placed to make.

No amount of interface work fixes that from outside. What fixes it is an owner inside the team, in its standups and its tools, with a sponsor who can say what done means.

What this means for how you buy design help

If the problem is organizational, the deliverable is downstream of a decision about who decides. That is why the engagements I run are shaped the way they are. When nobody can state the problem, the work is to get it stated in a sentence everyone recognizes, and to put something in front of the team concrete enough to judge together. When several teams need one foundation, the work is the foundation and the operating rules that keep it one. When an initiative has no owner, the work is to be the owner for a defined stretch and leave the team able to extend it.

In each case the screens get better. They get better because something behind them did.

The test I still use is whether the work keeps guiding people long after the project ends. Not whether the deliverables were good, but whether the team is better at this than it was.

If this is the situation you are in: Product Clarity, two to four weeks, one agreed problem. The account this draws on: One real-time view of a 450+ truck fleet.
