Who decides when software can act on its own?

The person accountable for the outcome, and the design work is making sure they really can. As AI moves from answering to acting, the interface sets when the system acts, when it asks, and when it leaves the choice alone.

In this article

  1. Designing an interface now means designing a decision
  2. A nudge and a push look the same in the metrics
  3. Aim for appropriate reliance, not maximum trust
  4. An approve button isn’t oversight
  5. What the system says when it doesn’t know belongs in the design system

Key idea 1 of 5

Designing an interface now means designing a decision

Here’s an illustration. Your retirement account’s assistant has worked out that raising your contribution by 3% would improve your outlook. Under that is a big button: Optimize my future.

One tap, and your next paycheck is smaller. Did you make an informed decision, or did someone make one decision feel inevitable? If it was wrong, who answers for it?

A good default can get someone saving who never would have started. The same levers can push a choice they wouldn’t make with the facts in front of them.

AI can tune those levers for each person, and it can move from suggesting to doing:

Each step down that list is a delegation, and the interface sets its terms. Every default, every framing and every button is part of the decision now.

Key idea 2 of 5

A nudge and a push look the same in the metrics

One version of that suggestion shows its assumptions and what it does to take-home pay, and leaves the choice to you. Another shows a large, reassuring button and puts the downsides in small print.

Both raise contributions. On a dashboard, they’re the same number.

We’ve gotten very good at measuring what people do. We’re much less practiced at asking whether they should have done it. And a system that knows your habits can also know when you’re most likely to say yes.

If a metric can’t tell a nudge from a push, it isn’t measuring the design.

Key idea 3 of 5

Aim for appropriate reliance, not maximum trust

The risk is also in what the person stops doing. Microsoft researchers found (opens in a new tab) that the more confident knowledge workers were in the AI, the less critical thinking they reported. It’s self-reported, so it shows a pattern, not a cause.

Explanations don’t fix it. In an earlier study (opens in a new tab), they made people more likely to go along with the AI, right or wrong. A fluent reason reads as a good one.

So I’d aim for what Microsoft’s researchers call appropriate reliance (opens in a new tab): people take the advice when it’s good and catch it when it isn’t.

Dispatch Cockpit, my prototype on synthetic data, tries to keep the checking alive:

  • Five figures from the records under each recommendation, not a score, because figures can be argued with
  • How old a stale figure is, and what to check before assigning
  • When two trucks are level, it says so and declines to pick
  • Skip weighs the same as Save, so disagreeing is cheap

Then measure the decision, not the acceptance. A high acceptance rate can mean the suggestions are good, or that people stopped reading them. More in Enterprise AI should help people decide, not just answer.

An explanation that sounds right is not evidence that it is right.

If saying no is harder than saying yes, the approval is a formality.

Key idea 4 of 5

An approve button isn’t oversight

An approval is only oversight if the person can actually say no.

Imagine a dispatch agent recommends moving a truck, with high confidence, and the dispatcher approves. The driver has a limitation that isn’t in the data. The math wasn’t wrong, but nothing on the screen said what was missing.

Now picture that all day. Accepting takes one click, and disagreeing takes several screens and a written reason. A person approved every action. Did they decide any of them?

The decision belongs to whoever is accountable for the outcome. In Dispatch Cockpit, the federal hours-of-service limit is checked before anything is ranked, and the close calls stay with the dispatcher.

The test is simple: give people a bad recommendation and count how many catch it. More in What does a person need to supervise an AI agent?

If saying no is harder than saying yes, the approval is a formality.

Key idea 5 of 5

What the system says when it doesn’t know belongs in the design system

A design system defines what an error looks like. An AI product also needs to decide what it says when it doesn’t know.

Ask, wait for a person, say how unsure it is, or refuse: those choices should be made once, in the system, not by each team on each screen.

A system that can say these four things is one I’d rely on. One that always has an answer is one I’d check every time.

Then measure whether people understood, how often they corrected the system, and whether they still feel in control. The rest is in What does a design system need once AI is in the product?

What a system says when it doesn’t know is a pattern, and patterns belong in the design system.

In one breath

As software moves from answering to acting, the interface sets the terms of a delegation. The person accountable for the outcome decides, and the design work is making sure they really can.

If you do one thing: find the one default in your product that decides for people. Test whether someone who disagreed would notice it, understand it and undo it in one step.
