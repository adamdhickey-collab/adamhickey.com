Who decides when software can act on its own?

The person accountable for the outcome, and the design work is making sure they really can. As AI moves from answering to acting, and from one recommendation to a run of steps, the interface sets when the system acts, when it asks, and what a person can check afterward.

Designing an interface now means designing a decision

Here’s an illustration. Your retirement account’s assistant has worked out that raising your contribution by 3% would improve your outlook. Under that is a big button: Optimize my future.

One tap, and your next paycheck is smaller. Did you make an informed decision, or did someone make one decision feel inevitable? If it was wrong, who answers for it?

A version that shows its assumptions and a version that hides the downsides in small print both raise contributions. On a dashboard, they’re the same number. If a metric can’t tell a nudge from a push, it isn’t measuring the design.

Each step down that list is a delegation, and the interface sets its terms. Every default, every framing, and every button is part of the decision now.

Aim for appropriate reliance, not maximum trust

The risk is also in what the person stops doing. Microsoft researchers found (opens in a new tab) that the more confident knowledge workers were in the AI, the less critical thinking they reported. It’s self-reported, so it shows a pattern, not a cause.

Explanations don’t fix it. In an earlier study (opens in a new tab), explanations made people more likely to go along with the AI, right or wrong. A fluent reason reads as a good one.

So I’d aim for what Microsoft’s researchers call appropriate reliance (opens in a new tab): people take the advice when it’s good and catch it when it isn’t.

Dispatch Cockpit, my prototype on synthetic data, tries to keep the checking alive:

  • Five figures from the records under each recommendation, not a score, because figures can be argued with
  • How old a stale figure is, and what to check before assigning
  • When two trucks are level, it says so and declines to pick
  • Skip weighs the same as Save, so disagreeing is cheap

Then measure the decision, not the acceptance. A high acceptance rate can mean the suggestions are good, or that people stopped reading them.

An explanation that sounds right is not evidence that it is right.

An agent is a run of decisions, so draw the line where a mistake gets expensive

In Dispatch Cockpit a person judges one recommendation: one load, one truck, one press. An agent is given a goal instead, and takes a run of steps nobody watches one by one.

Picture an agent working the overnight queue of failed orders. By morning it has touched forty. The specialist who arrives at seven needs to know:

  • Which of the forty it was sure about
  • Which it wasn’t
  • What it did when it wasn’t
  • Whether any of that can still be undone

The first design decision is made off the screen: the line between what the agent may do alone and what it has to ask about. I’d draw it where a mistake gets expensive to reverse, not where it gets possible.

The person supervising the agent has to see where the line is, and move it for one partner or one kind of order, without opening a ticket.

Autonomy is a boundary in the workflow, and engineering has to enforce it where the copy describes it.

If saying no is harder than saying yes, the approval is a formality.

An approve button isn’t oversight

When a system reaches the line, it asks, and how it asks is most of the interface. “Resubmit order 4471?” teaches people to answer yes, because there’s nothing in it to judge.

The question that works carries its evidence: this order failed on a missing customer identifier, eleven others from this partner have had the same gap since Tuesday, and here is the corrected document beside the original, with the change marked.

Two more things belong on it and are usually missing: what happens if nobody answers at three in the morning, and whether a yes covers this order or teaches the agent to stop asking.

The decision belongs to whoever is accountable for the outcome. In Dispatch Cockpit, the federal hours-of-service limit is checked before anything is ranked, and the close calls stay with the dispatcher.

The test is simple: give people a bad recommendation and count how many catch it. If saying no is harder than saying yes, the approval is a formality.

Design psychology

When in doubt, do less, and say so

The one human–AI guideline I’d keep is to scope the service when in doubt: ask or do less, rather than guess and do more. A step held for a person is cheaper than a confident wrong one.

The guidelines, with the research behind each: Microsoft, Guidelines for Human-AI Interaction

The run has to be readable afterward, by someone who wasn’t there

Readable is a stronger word than logged. The specialist at seven needs the forty grouped: the three that hit the line and waited at the top, the one it skipped next, and the thirty-six it resubmitted folded away.

Stopping is where the record matters most. Stopping before the next step is easy, and undoing the last one depends on the record. Handing a half-done queue back to a person is the stop nobody designs.

If a decision can’t be reconstructed from the record, the agent wasn’t supervised. It was trusted. An agent that finishes everything and is never caught is either perfect or unsupervised, and the dashboard can’t tell you which.

What the system says when it doesn’t know belongs in the design system

A design system defines what an error looks like. An AI product also needs to decide what it says when it doesn’t know.

Ask, wait for a person, say how unsure it is, or refuse: decide those once, in the system, not on each screen.

A system that can say these four things is one I’d rely on. One that always has an answer is one I’d check every time.

What a system says when it doesn’t know is a pattern, and patterns belong in the design system.
