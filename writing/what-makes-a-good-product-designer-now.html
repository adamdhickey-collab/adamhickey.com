What makes a good product designer now?

Designing the system every builder works from, person or agent, and deciding what it must never decide alone. Coding agents can build a working interface in an afternoon, so the scarce work is behind the screen.

In this article

  2. A design system now has to explain itself to builders who can’t ask
  3. Build to find out, not to produce more
  4. Decide what software does alone, and where a person decides

Key idea 1 of 5

Building got fast, so a bad decision travels further

For most of my career, design ended at the handoff. I drew the blueprint, someone else built it, and months later we found out what survived.

Now I build what I design, with coding agents, and a working version of an idea takes days instead of a quarter. That sounds like a story about speed. I think it’s a story about where the mistakes go.

When building is cheap, the expensive mistakes move. A badly drawn screen used to cost one screen, and someone caught it in review. A badly written rule now repeats on every screen an agent builds from it.

So the work moved too, into what the building is made from. What’s new is that the next builder may not be a person.

Key idea 2 of 5

A design system now has to explain itself to builders who can’t ask

A new designer learns a design system from its pages and from the people around them. When something is unclear, they ask. An agent builds from what’s written down, and it doesn’t ask.

Netflix’s design system team (opens in a new tab) now counts AI agents among the system’s users, and warns that “left unchecked, AI can quietly drift from the system’s rules.”

In Agent Review, my prototype for supervising coding agents, a simulated job moves billing screens onto the design system. Every action becomes the system’s Button except “Cancel subscription”: small grey text, built by hand.

Is it quiet on purpose? The checks pass whichever way the agent goes. The answer isn’t in the code, so no check can say which fix is right.

A component that only says how it looks will be used everywhere it looks right. One that says what it means, and where it doesn’t apply, can be used well by a builder who has never met the team.

Key idea 3 of 5

Build to find out, not to produce more

The time fast building saves can go to making more, or to learning faster. I think only the second is the job.

Lucy Learns, the dog-training app I built for our household, shipped with voice commands. Say “next step” and the app moved on. It worked: the words were heard, and the steps turned.

Then we used it in the hallway, and Lucy heard every command as meant for her. The AI I built it with didn’t see that coming, and neither did I. A couple of real sessions did, and listening came out.

A working prototype is a question, asked in a form people can answer. It should end in a call, made on what people did with it.

When building is cheap, the expensive mistakes move.

Key idea 4 of 5

Decide what software does alone, and where a person decides

Software used to wait for someone to press the button. Now it can take the action itself, and where it stops to ask is a design decision.

Agent Review sorts an agent’s work into three kinds, and a person only ever sees two of them.

The third kind is where oversight quietly goes wrong. Picture someone approving fifty of these a day, each with a green check and one button. Are they deciding, or clicking?

An approve button isn’t oversight. If the person approving can’t see the evidence, the consequence and a way to say no, their yes is a formality.

Key idea 5 of 5

It’s the job I started with, at a longer reach

I started in 2005 as a hybrid designer and developer, when designing and building were one job. Then the jobs split, and I spent thirteen years at RBA on enterprise products, and on a UX practice inside a global enterprise.

That’s where I learned that coherence at scale takes systems. Nobody reviews every screen when many hands build, and now some of the hands are agents.

So quality has to live in checks, from the SAP program to this site:

A check is a design decision that keeps being made after the designer has left the room.

This year the two halves came back together. I design the system good work comes from, build enough of it to know it holds, and decide, with the people who own the product, where a person stays in charge.

None of this makes the craft matter less. A check can hold the spacing. It can’t tell you whether the thing is worth using, so someone still has to go and look.

What I’d ask a designer now:

  • Can they say what a component means, not only how it looks?
  • Have they built something real, and changed their mind because of it?
  • Can they say where an AI feature should stop and ask?
  • Do they write the checks, or only pass them?
  • Do they still go and watch someone use the thing?

In one breath

Building got easy, so a bad decision travels further. The job now is the infrastructure behind great design, and knowing where a person decides.

If you do one thing: pick the component your team uses most and write two sentences: what it means, and where it must not be used. Then hand them to the next builder, person or agent.
