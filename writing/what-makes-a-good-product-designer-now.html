What makes a good product designer now?

Designing the system every builder works from, person or agent, and deciding what it must never decide alone. Coding agents can build a working interface in an afternoon, so the scarce work is behind the screen: what each part means, the checks every change must pass, and where a person decides. Twenty years in enterprise software turned out to be the training for it.

Key idea 1 of 6

The building got fast, so the judgment moved upstream

For most of my career, design ended at the handoff. I drew the blueprint, someone else built it, and months later we found out what survived. Now I build what I design, with coding agents, and a working version of an idea takes days instead of a quarter.

When building is cheap, the expensive mistakes move. A badly drawn screen used to cost one screen. A badly written rule now repeats across every screen an agent touches. So the designer’s leverage moved upstream, into what the building is made from: the parts, the rules, the checks, and the decisions only a person should make.

None of that is new to me. It’s what I spent thirteen years building inside enterprise programs. What’s new is that the next builder may not be a person.

Key idea 2 of 6

A design system now has to explain itself to builders who can’t ask

A new designer learns a design system from its documentation and from the people around them. An agent builds from what’s written down, faster than any review can keep up with.

Ben Lister and Szymon Chudy (opens in a new tab) count AI agents among the users of Hawkins Professional, Netflix’s enterprise design system, and warn that “left unchecked, AI can quietly drift from the system’s rules.” Their answer is a paved road (opens in a new tab): skills, MCP servers and context shipped with the system. Atlassian’s design system team (opens in a new tab) calls the same work a context engine.

My own example is a red. In Agent Review, my prototype for supervising coding agents, the same red marks “Payment failed” on one screen and a replaced plan on another. Every check passes. What the red means isn’t in the code, so no check can say which fix is right.

A component that only says how it looks will be used everywhere it looks right. One that says what it means and where it doesn’t apply can be used correctly by a builder who has never met the team.

Key idea 3 of 6

Build to find out, not to produce more

The time fast building saves can go to making more, or to learning faster. Only the second is the job.

Ryan Brownlow’s account of designing Korl (opens in a new tab), an AI product for sales presentations, shows how. He prototyped with Figma’s MCP server and Claude Code, and testing showed that people’s hand edits broke the presentation’s sync with its data. The answer was one source of truth for every piece of tracked data, not better generation.

Lucy Learns, a dog-training app I built, shipped with voice commands. A couple of real sessions took them out: the dog heard every word spoken at the phone as meant for her. The AI I built it with didn’t flag that. Using it did.

A working prototype is a question, asked in a form people can answer. It should end in a call, made on what people did with it, not on how fast it came together.

When building is cheap, the expensive mistakes move.

Key idea 4 of 6

Decide what software does alone, and where a person decides

Software used to wait for people to press the button. Now it can take the action itself, and the line between what it does alone and what a person decides is a design decision.

Tushar Roy’s case study (opens in a new tab) of security metrics at Apple draws that line well. When a metric breaks its limits, an agent drafts the analysis with its reasoning and a confidence score, and it stays a draft until the person accountable signs off.

The target isn’t trust. Microsoft researchers argue for appropriate reliance (opens in a new tab): accepting a recommendation when it’s right and rejecting it when it isn’t. Explanations don’t guarantee that. In one study (opens in a new tab), they made people accept the AI more whether it was right or not.

Agent Review sorts an agent’s work into three lanes, and a person only ever sees two of them.

An approve button isn’t oversight. If the person approving can’t see the evidence, the consequence and a way to say no, their yes is a formality.

Key idea 5 of 6

Quality has to hold when nobody is looking

When agents and many hands build, nobody reviews every screen. Quality has to live in checks that run on every change.

GitHub’s Primer design system (opens in a new tab) ships its checks with the system. Its MCP server hands an agent the components and their guidelines, plus a token linter and an alt-text review, so the agent meets them while it builds, not after.

This site is plain HTML built with coding agents, and ten checks run on every merge, five of them in a real browser. On the SAP program, the same idea was a quality check in the release process across more than forty apps.

A check is a design decision that keeps being made after the designer has left the room. Writing it is part of the design.

Key idea 6 of 6

It’s the job I started with, at a longer reach

I started in 2005 as a hybrid designer and developer, when designing and building were one job. Then the jobs split, and I spent thirteen years at RBA on enterprise products for CBRE, Toro, Banner Bank and International Trucks, and building a UX practice inside a global enterprise. That’s where I learned that coherence at scale takes systems.

This year the two halves came back together. I design the system good work comes from, I build enough of it to know it holds, and I decide, with the people who own the product, where a person stays in charge.

What to ask a designer now:

  • Can they say what a component means, not only how it looks?
  • Have they built something real, and changed their mind because of it?
  • Can they say where an AI feature should stop and ask?
  • Do they write the checks, or only pass them?
  • Do they still go and watch someone use the thing?

In one breath

Coding agents made building fast, so the designer’s leverage moved to what the building is made from: parts that say what they mean, checks every change passes, and a clear line where a person decides. Good designers now build to find out, and design the infrastructure behind great design.

If you do one thing: pick the component your team uses most and write two sentences: what it means, and where it must not be used. Then hand them to the next builder, person or agent.
