What should a working prototype actually prove?

Whatever makes the next investment risky, which is usually whether the idea is worth building. A prototype uses real code, real inputs, and the people who’d use it, and ends in a build, kill, or park call.

In this article

  1. The demo answers the wrong question
  2. Building got fast, so judgment got expensive
  3. Real code, real inputs, a real Tuesday
  4. A hallway found what no demo would have

Key idea 1 of 5

The demo answers the wrong question

The team has ideas: sketches, a deck, half a decision, maybe a demo or three, and pressure to make one of them real. Nobody can say yet which is worth building, because nothing exists anyone can use.

A demo doesn’t fix that. A demo proves the idea can be demonstrated, which was never in doubt. The questions that decide whether to fund it are different:

  • Does anyone reach for this on an ordinary day?
  • What happens when the inputs are the real ones rather than the ones on the slide?
  • What does it cost to keep running once the person who built the demo has moved on?

Sometimes the risky question is technical: can two systems talk to each other at all? More often the idea can plainly be built, and the open question is whether it should be.

Key idea 2 of 5

Building got fast, so judgment got expensive

On Lucy Learns, the dog-training app this article comes back to, AI built working versions in an afternoon. Repainting every illustration took an afternoon too, where a month earlier it would have taken five more days.

If a prototype that took two weeks now takes two days, the question becomes: did we prototype the right thing? I think the scarce part is knowing which questions matter, and when to stop generating and commit.

So a prototype should be judged by the decision it produced, not by how fast it appeared.

Key idea 3 of 5

Real code, real inputs, a real Tuesday

Three things separate a prototype that proves something from one that merely exists. It runs in real code, so the constraints that will decide the product show up now rather than in month four.

It runs on real inputs, the messy ones. And it’s used by the people who would use it on a Tuesday, where they’d use it, not by the sponsor in a conference room.

The last one is where most prototypes stop short, and it’s the one that answers the question.

A demo proves the idea can be demonstrated, which was never in doubt.

Key idea 4 of 5

A hallway found what no demo would have

I built Lucy Learns for our dog. The trainer’s handouts were written for someone sitting still, and practice happens in a hallway with a leash in one hand.

Voice commands came out after a couple of real sessions, because Lucy heard every command as meant for her. Then the timer that replaced them broke on the pauses real sessions are full of.

No demo would have found either. A hallway did. The next version is a teleprompter, and it isn’t built yet.

The pictures went through a blind pilot: four language models read them shuffled, with the filenames hidden, and the two they all misread were redrawn. The first versions looked finished. Finished isn’t the same as understood.

Design psychology

A finished surface is read as a true one

People judge how right something is partly by how easily it’s processed, so a polished mockup feels truer than a rough sketch before anyone has checked. A working thing in the right hands is harder to mistake for finished.

On processing fluency and judgment: Alter and Oppenheimer, Uniting the Tribes of Fluency

Key idea 5 of 5

A prototype ends in one of three calls, with the next cost attached

There are three honest calls:

  • Build: it works, people reached for it, and here is what the next step costs.
  • Kill: it didn’t, and here is what we learned that stops us spending on it again.
  • Park: the idea is sound and the moment is wrong, and here is what would have to change.

A prototype that talks a team out of something expensive has paid for itself. The cheapest product decision a team can make is the one it makes before the build starts.

AI is part of how the thing gets built, and sometimes part of the idea. It isn’t what the prototype is for, and a prototype that flatters the tools that made it is a demo again.

In one breath

A prototype should resolve whatever makes the next investment risky, and end in a build, kill, or park call with the cost of the next step attached.

If you do one thing: put the prototype in the hands of one person who would use it on a Tuesday. Do it where they would use it, before anyone shows it to the sponsor.
