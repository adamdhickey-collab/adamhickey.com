Where does design end and development begin, now?

For most of my career the line was easy to see. Design happened over here, development over there, and eventually there was a handoff. Now I can describe an interface to an AI coding tool, give it a design system, inspect what it creates, change the behavior, connect data and deploy the result. The line did not disappear. It moved, from what a job title says to a better question: what can I build, verify, and responsibly stand behind?

Key idea 1 of 7

The old boundary was drawn by the cost of production, and production got cheap

The traditional division of labor made sense. Designers designed interfaces, developers wrote code, and each discipline required specialized tools and specialized knowledge. Building software was expensive enough that organizations assembled teams around it.

AI has compressed some of those boundaries. It is increasingly possible for one experienced person to move across several disciplines without becoming equally expert in all of them.

That last phrase matters. I do not have to become a career software engineer to build useful software. But I also should not confuse the ability to produce code with deep engineering expertise. AI can help me cross a knowledge gap. It does not automatically eliminate the gap.

So the interesting question is not whether a designer can now build. Increasingly, yes. It is where the responsible line sits, and it has less to do with whether something is called front end or back end than I would have guessed.

Key idea 2 of 7

Build is not the same as verify

This may be the most important distinction in the whole question.

AI dramatically increases my ability to build things I could not previously build. But every increase in building power creates a corresponding verification problem.

Suppose an AI tool generates an authentication flow. It runs. I can sign in. Wonderful. But can someone access another user’s account? What happens when a session expires? Are keys exposed? What happens when someone sends malformed data on purpose, or fifty thousand requests arrive at once?

The interface can look perfect while the implementation underneath contains serious problems. As a designer I am trained to see many categories of failure: confusion, accessibility, hierarchy, workflow errors, cognitive load. A senior engineer sees additional categories: race conditions, security vulnerabilities, data corruption, cache invalidation, concurrency.

I do not need to become paranoid about every project. I need to recognize when the invisible failure modes have become more important than the visible ones. That is where the line begins.

Key idea 3 of 7

Can I build it, can I verify it, can I own the failure

Before deciding whether to own a technical implementation myself, I ask three things.

Can I build it?

With my current skills and AI assistance, can I create the thing? Increasingly the answer is yes. That is only question one.

Can I verify it?

Do I know enough to determine whether the implementation is correct? Not merely that it appears to work, but that I understand the important ways it could fail and know how to test for them. When that answer starts becoming uncertain, I have entered the territory where someone else should look.

Can I responsibly own the consequences if it fails?

If a portfolio site’s animation breaks, we fix it. If a personal prototype loses some test data, that is annoying and manageable. If a healthcare application exposes private patient information, that is a completely different situation. The consequence changes the level of engineering confidence required.

So the real line is this: I can own the implementation as long as I can reasonably verify it and the consequences of failure stay within a level I am qualified to accept. That is a much better rule than designers do not do back ends.

Key idea 4 of 7

The line sits where the invisible failure modes overtake the visible ones

There is a large territory I am comfortable owning: the product thinking, the user experience, the visual system, the front end, the prototype, the design system, content structure, a straightforward content management system, simple data interactions, and integrations with mature services used according to their intended patterns. In that territory the hard part is rarely computer science. It is product judgment: what the interface should do, what someone sees first, what becomes a reusable component, where complexity belongs.

And there is a territory where I want an experienced engineer involved, not because AI cannot generate code for it but because the cost of being subtly wrong is too high: sensitive personal data, regulated or financial systems beyond straightforward use of established providers, complex authorization, real-time synchronization at scale, migrations of important production data, anything with uptime guarantees.

A practical tell: if I hear myself think I think this is secure, that sentence is the signal to bring someone in. Security is a bad area for casual confidence.

Design psychology

Confidence is not competence, and fluency hides the difference

The less someone knows about a field, the less able they are to see what they are missing in it, because the skill that would show them is the skill they lack. That is not a flaw in the person; it is the shape of expertise from the outside. An authentication flow that runs and lets you sign in feels complete, and the categories of failure a security engineer would see are exactly the ones a designer has no instrument for. The tell is a sentence that sounds like confidence and is actually the absence of a test.

The original study: Kruger and Dunning, Unskilled and Unaware of It

Key idea 5 of 7

Bring engineers in for leverage, not rescue

I do not want the relationship with developers to be: I tried to build this until I got stuck, and now I need someone to save it. That is a bad collaboration.

The better shape is to know early when another person’s expertise creates leverage. I design and build most of an application. Then a senior engineer reviews the architecture, the security, the permissions and the deployment. Maybe that engineer spends ten hours rather than ten weeks. I keep ownership of the product experience and most of the implementation, and the client gets specialized judgment exactly where it matters.

Sometimes the engineer should arrive at the beginning, because the client’s central challenge is technical: can these systems integrate, can the data architecture support this, can we migrate off the existing platform. If the answer to one of those determines the product, engineering belongs in the first conversation. Do not wait until the end to discover the constraint that should have shaped the beginning.

Key idea 6 of 7

One person carries the product; a specialist joins where it matters

Every engagement I run now takes one of three shapes. I design it. I design and build it. Or I lead it and bring in engineering for the parts where deeper expertise matters, while staying the primary relationship and the product lead.

Both of these statements are true: I can build, and I know when someone better than me should own part of the system. The second increases the credibility of the first.

From a client’s side, that can be considerably simpler than hiring a strategist, a UX designer, a UI designer, a front-end developer, a project manager and an AI consultant. One very experienced person understands the problem, maintains continuity, and makes the idea tangible quickly. When the project genuinely requires deeper engineering, that specialist joins.

Key idea 7 of 7

Never bluff the invisible parts

I can experiment aggressively, learn new technologies and let AI take me into unfamiliar territory. But when client money, customer data, security or important business operations are involved, I do not pretend to a certainty I do not have. If I do not know, I say: I want an engineer to review this part.

That sentence should not feel like a failure. Expertise includes recognizing the limits of expertise.

The old model was: I design, then I hand it to development. The new one is: I carry the product as far as it makes sense for me to carry it. Sometimes that is the whole way. Sometimes someone walks beside me for part of it. The question I draw the line with is no longer what a UX designer was traditionally supposed to do. It is what I can build, verify, and responsibly stand behind. Everything before that line is room to expand. Everything beyond it is a reason to collaborate.

In one breath

The line moved from what a job title says to what one person can build, verify and responsibly stand behind. Every gain in building power is a verification problem the same size, and engineers come in for leverage, early, rather than for rescue at the end.

If you do one thing: the next time you hear yourself think “I think this is secure”, stop there and book the review.
