The payment was sent. Did the supplier process it?

An accounts payable specialist asks: “We sent a virtual payment for this invoice. Has the supplier processed it, and should I follow up?” One payment, followed from the buyer’s list to the supplier’s notice and back to the same record, as a working prototype and the design work behind it.

The problem
“Paid” hides three facts: the buyer sent a payment, the supplier processed it, and the buyer matched it to an invoice. A specialist asked which is true has to read the history to find out.
What’s different
The record leads with the two facts that can differ, then who acts next and the one thing needed to act. The history is there, folded under them.
What I did
The task model, the information architecture, the content hierarchy, the service map, the states, and the research plan. Then I built the prototype in code, with Claude Code, inside this site’s own system.
What it shows
A direction worked through far enough to test with accounts payable specialists. Nothing yet about which events a real system can promise: the test I’d run first is below.

Independent commercial payments concept · Simulated data. Wren Payments, Fieldstone Foods, and Larkspur Packaging are made up, every reference is synthetic, and nothing here is any bank’s screen or procedure.

A supplier calls, and “paid” turns out to be three different things

Thursday afternoon. Larkspur Packaging calls accounts payable about invoice INV-20483. The specialist opens the payment: sent October 28. So it’s paid.

Except nothing says whether Larkspur has processed it. The invoice is still open in the books, because nobody has matched it. And the payment has a date it stops being good on.

Three facts, one word. The data for all three usually exists. I think the design problem is the hierarchy: keeping the facts apart, and saying who moves next.

One invoice reference runs through four screens

The buyer never leaves their own record. The supplier sees one notice. The reference ties them, and the two events that matter happen at different ends.

Status first, history second, and why that order is worth testing

Both versions hold the same facts. The first asks the specialist to work the state out from the events. The second states it, and risks being wrong when an event is missing.

Why test it rather than ship it. A stated status is only as good as the event behind it. If “Waiting for supplier” shows while a processing event sits in a queue, v2 is confidently wrong where v1 was merely slow. The test below asks whether the stated fact, with its last confirmed event beside it, is trusted and correct more often, and whether anyone misses the log.

What was intended, how it’s built, and what checked it

v1 and v2 are the two hierarchies above; v2 is the working direction the prototype builds. There is no Figma source. The artifacts are this page’s own HTML, the fixture at the top of wren.js, and the test that walks it.

Where the concept comes from, and what it doesn’t claim

U.S. Bank Access Online

Describes a card program management tool whose functions include virtual payments, beside account setup, transaction management, and reporting.

The question it raises: inside a card management workspace, where does a payment’s handoff to the supplier become visible?

U.S. Bank Virtual Pay

Describes buyer-to-supplier payments that carry detailed remittance information, each payment a unique identifier.

The question it raises: if the invoice reference travels with the payment, can it be the one thing both sides search by?

American Express vPayment

Describes a supplier charging a virtual account, then the transaction data reconciled against the buyer’s predefined details.

The question it raises: processing and reconciliation are two events there too. Should a buyer’s screen show them as two facts?

The screen sequence, the event names, who may see what, and how a match behaves here are my design assumptions. They are not Access Online’s screens or procedures, or any bank’s.

What I’d test first, and the decision it would settle

The decision: whether a next-action summary belongs above the activity history. v2 bets that it does. If specialists read the summary, trust it, and still open the log when it matters, it stays. If they read past it to the log anyway, v1 was right and the summary is noise.

ProposedThe test

With accounts payable
Five specialists, each trying both hierarchies on the same five payments: find INV-20483, say whether the supplier has processed it, say who acts next, then handle the expired one.
With a supplier
Three accounts receivable people, walked through the notice: which invoice is this for, and what would you do with it?
Measured
Correct status interpretation, time to find the invoice, and any attempted action that would duplicate a payment or replace one early.
With partners
Engineering: which events and timestamps are reliable. Content design: which terms buyers already use. Accessibility and compliance: which states, controls, and data boundaries need review.

Checked in the buildWhat the prototype does

  • Process and Confirm match can’t be pressed twice, and a match leaves the processing event alone
  • Every screen moves focus, announces itself, and names each status in words and a shape

A day to build, thirteen years to know which fact to put first

The prototype took a day with Claude Code, built inside this site’s own tokens, with its checks run on every state. The agent built what I decided. The decisions came first, and they came from thirteen years of enterprise workflows where people got lost between systems that each worked on their own.

What I’d still need to learn is the payments operation itself: which events a bank’s systems can promise, what a supplier’s receivables desk actually does with a notice, and where compliance draws the line on what a buyer may show.

What I’d bring is the part that didn’t get easier: deciding which fact goes first, and making it real enough to test.
