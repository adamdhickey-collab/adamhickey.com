The payment was sent. Did the supplier process it?

An accounts payable specialist gets a call about an invoice marked paid. One payment, followed from the buyer’s list to the supplier’s notice and back, as a working prototype and the thinking behind it.

The problem
“Paid” hides three facts: the buyer sent a payment, the supplier processed it, and the buyer matched it to an invoice. A specialist has to read the history to find out which is true.
The decision
Lead with the two facts that can differ, then who acts next. The history stays, folded underneath.
What I did
The hierarchy, the flow, and the states. Then I built the prototype in code, with Claude Code, inside this site’s own system.
What it shows
A direction worked through far enough to test with accounts payable specialists. No findings yet: the test I’d run first is below.

A supplier calls, and “paid” turns out to be three different things

Thursday afternoon. Larkspur Packaging calls accounts payable about invoice INV-20483. The specialist opens the payment: sent October 28. So it’s paid.

Except “paid” is standing in for three facts, and the screen says one of them.

The data for all three usually exists. I think the design problem is the hierarchy: keeping the facts apart, and saying who moves next.

Six decisions, and the reason behind each

None of these needed new data. Each one is about which fact comes first, in whose words, and what stays out of the way.

  1. Two facts, not one status

    The record opens on “Supplier processing” and “Invoice matching” as two separate facts, each named for who owns it, each with one line on where it stands.

    Why: one word has to be true for the buyer, the supplier, and the books at once. Two facts can each be checked, and can differ without either one lying.

  2. Say who acts next, and give them one button

    Under the facts, one line names who moves next: you, the supplier, or no one. If there is something to do, the one button for it sits right there.

    Why: the specialist’s question is “do I follow up?” A screen that answers it outright beats one that leaves it to be worked out from a log.

  3. Let the invoice reference carry the handoff

    INV-20483 is a column on the list, the title of the record, the headline of the supplier’s notice, and the thing the match is checked against.

    Why: the supplier knows the invoice and the buyer knows the invoice. Neither of them knows the payment’s own number.

  4. Show the supplier only what the buyer would share

    The notice carries what the supplier needs to process it: the buyer, the invoice, the amount, and the date it stops being good. Not the approver, the coding, or the notes.

    Why: the handoff crosses two companies. What the buyer wrote for itself stays on the buyer’s side of it.

  5. Keep the history, folded

    Every event is still on the record, under “Activity,” closed by default. The buyer’s record and the supplier’s notice are drawn from the same list of events, so the two can’t disagree.

    Why: the log is the evidence, not the answer. It has to stay one press away for the day the stated answer looks wrong.

  6. A status is words and a shape before it is a color

    Not processed, processed, ready, matched, and expired each get a word and a mark: a clock, a check, an open circle, a double check, a triangle. Color comes third, and expired is the only one in the caution ink.

    Why: a row has to read in a long list, on a phone, through a screen reader, and to someone who can’t see red. The word does that on its own. The color only helps.

One invoice reference runs through four screens

The buyer never leaves their own record. The supplier sees one notice. The reference ties them, and the two events that matter happen at different ends.

Status first, history second, and why that order is worth testing

Larkspur is on the phone. Both records below hold the same facts. Try answering from each.

Why test it rather than ship it. A stated status is only as good as the event behind it. If a processing event sits in a queue, v2 is confidently wrong where v1 was merely slow. So v2 says when it last checked.

  • The test: people try both records, half in each order. Find INV-20483, say whether the supplier has processed it, say who acts next, then handle the expired one.
  • Measured: correct status readings, time to find the invoice, and any press that would duplicate a payment or replace one early.
  • Findings: none yet. No specialist or supplier has used this, and nothing on this page is a result.

Where the concept comes from, and what it doesn’t claim

Three kinds of public product description, and the question each one raised for this design.

The screen sequence, the event names, who may see what, and how a match behaves here are my design assumptions, not any bank’s screens or procedures.

Card program management tools

Bank portals whose functions include virtual payments, beside account setup, transaction management, and reporting.

Inside a card management workspace, where does a payment’s handoff to the supplier become visible?

Virtual payment programs

Buyer-to-supplier payments that carry detailed remittance information, each payment a unique identifier.

If the invoice reference travels with the payment, can it be the one thing both sides search by?

Supplier-charged virtual accounts

In American Express vPayment, a supplier charges a virtual account, then the transaction data is reconciled against the buyer’s predefined details.

Processing and reconciliation are two events there too. Should a buyer’s screen show them as two facts?

A day to build, thirteen years to know which fact to put first

The prototype took a day with Claude Code, inside this site’s own tokens. A test script walks the handoff and confirms Process and Confirm match can’t be pressed twice, and the site’s checks measure all twelve of its reachable states.

The agent built what I decided. The decisions came first, from thirteen years of enterprise workflows where people got lost between systems that each worked on their own.

What this page can’t settle: which events a bank’s systems can promise, what a supplier’s receivables desk does with a notice, and where compliance draws the line on what a buyer may show.

An agent will build whichever record you ask for. Deciding which fact goes first is still the hard part.
