The invoice says paid. Why is the supplier still calling?

The buyer’s system says paid because it issued a virtual card. The supplier says unpaid because nobody has charged it. I designed a payment record that says what happened, who acts next, and what to do now.

What was hard
“Paid” means a card was issued, nothing more. Whether the supplier charged it, and whether the buyer matched it, the screen never showed.
What I decided
The card is issued. So the record leads with the two questions still open: has the supplier charged it, and has the buyer matched it? Then who acts next.
Why it matters
A specialist who can’t tell has to guess: a follow-up nobody needed, or a second payment on top of a good one.
What I did
The hierarchy, the flow, and the states, then the prototype in code with Claude Code. No findings yet: the test I’d run first is below.

Larkspur calls, and the screen settles one fact of three

Thursday afternoon. Larkspur Packaging, the supplier, calls Fieldstone Foods, the buyer: “Our records still show INV-20483 as unpaid. Can you check the payment?” The specialist opens it. Sent October 28, marked paid.

“Paid” is standing in for three facts. I think the design problem is the hierarchy: keep them apart, say who moves next, and give the specialist a line for the phone.

Three decisions carry the concept, and three more support them

  1. Two facts, not one status

    The record opens on “Supplier processing” and “Invoice matching,” each named for who owns it.

    Why: two facts can differ without either one lying.

  2. Answer the call first, then the follow-up

    “What to tell them” leads: the card hasn’t been charged, who got the notice, and a button to send it again. The follow-up date sits under it.

    Why: the supplier is on the phone now. A reminder for the 20th doesn’t end the call.

  3. Show the supplier only what the buyer would share

    The notice carries the buyer, the invoice, the amount, and the last good date. Not the approver, the coding, or the notes.

    Why: the handoff crosses two companies.

Three more, in support:

  • Let the invoice reference carry the handoff. INV-20483 titles the record, heads the notice, and is what the match checks against.
  • Keep the history, folded. Every event stays under “Activity,” closed by default; both views read the same simulated events.
  • Words and a shape before a color. Each status gets a word and a mark, for screen readers and for anyone who can’t see red.

One invoice reference runs through four screens

From a log to an answer, and why that order is worth testing

Larkspur is on the phone. Both records come from the same events; v2 adds the matching status, who acts next, and what to say. Answer from each.

A stated status is only as good as the event behind it. If a charge sits in a queue, v2 is confidently wrong where v1 was merely slow. So v2 dates its “no,” and the expired record asks for one more check before a replacement.

  • The test: half in each order: find the invoice, read its status, say who acts next, and handle the expired one, without the walkthrough.
  • Measured: correct status readings, time to find the invoice, and any press that would duplicate a payment.
  • Findings: none yet.

Where the concept comes from, and what it doesn’t claim

The concept organizes simulated events into a status summary and next-step guidance. The screens, events, permissions, and matching are my assumptions, not any bank’s procedures, and a production version would check the events and the business rules first.

A day to build, thirteen years to know which fact to put first

The prototype took a day with Claude Code, on this site’s own tokens. A test script holds its safeguard: Process and Confirm match can’t be pressed twice here. A real system has a harder job stopping a duplicate.

The agent built what I decided. The decisions came from thirteen years of enterprise workflows.

What this page can’t settle: which events a bank’s systems can promise, what a supplier’s receivables desk does with a notice, and where compliance draws the line.

An agent will build whichever record you ask for. Deciding which fact goes first is still the hard part.
