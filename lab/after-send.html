The payment was sent. Did the supplier process it?

A supplier calls about an invoice marked paid. The status is accurate, and the accounts payable specialist still can’t tell what to do next.

What was hard
“Paid” hides three facts: sent, processed, and matched. A specialist has to read the history to find out which is true.
What I decided
Lead with the two facts that can differ, then who acts next. The history stays, folded underneath.
Why it matters
A specialist who can’t tell has to guess: a follow-up nobody needed, or a second payment on top of a good one.
What I did
The hierarchy, the flow, and the states, then the prototype in code with Claude Code. No findings yet: the test I’d run first is below.

A supplier calls, and “paid” turns out to be three different things

Thursday afternoon. Larkspur Packaging calls accounts payable about invoice INV-20483. The specialist opens the payment: sent October 28. So it’s paid.

“Paid” is standing in for three facts, and the screen says one of them.

I think the design problem is the hierarchy: keeping the facts apart, and saying who moves next.

Six decisions, and the reason behind each

None of these needed new data. The one I’d highlight: saying who acts next.

  1. Two facts, not one status

    The record opens on “Supplier processing” and “Invoice matching,” each named for who owns it.

    Why: two facts can differ without either one lying.

  2. Say who acts next, with one button

    One line names who moves next: you, the supplier, or no one. Anything to do is one button.

    Why: the specialist’s question is “do I follow up?” A screen that answers it outright beats a log.

  3. Let the invoice reference carry the handoff

    INV-20483 titles the record, heads the supplier’s notice, and is what the match is checked against.

    Why: both sides know the invoice. Neither knows the payment’s own number.

  4. Show the supplier only what the buyer would share

    The notice carries the buyer, the invoice, the amount, and the last good date. Not the approver, the coding, or the notes.

    Why: the handoff crosses two companies.

  5. Keep the history, folded

    Every event stays on the record, under “Activity,” closed by default. Both sides draw from the same list of events, so they can’t disagree.

    Why: the log is the evidence, not the answer. It stays one press away for the day the answer looks wrong.

  6. Words and a shape before a color

    Each status gets a word and a mark. Color comes third.

    Why: a row has to read through a screen reader, and to someone who can’t see red. The word does that on its own.

One invoice reference runs through four screens

Status first, history second, and why that order is worth testing

Larkspur is on the phone. Both records hold the same facts. Try answering from each.

Why test it rather than ship it. A stated status is only as good as the event behind it. If a processing event sits in a queue, v2 is confidently wrong where v1 was merely slow. So v2 says when it last checked.

  • The test: half in each order: find the invoice, read its status, say who acts next, and handle the expired one.
  • Measured: correct status readings, time to find the invoice, and any press that would duplicate a payment.
  • Findings: none yet.

Where the concept comes from, and what it doesn’t claim

Three kinds of public product description, and the question each raised.

The screens, events, permissions, and matching here are my assumptions, not any bank’s procedures.

Supplier-charged virtual accounts

Processing and reconciliation are two events there too. Should a buyer see two facts?

A day to build, thirteen years to know which fact to put first

The prototype took a day with Claude Code, on this site’s own tokens. A test script confirms Process and Confirm match can’t be pressed twice.

The agent built what I decided. The decisions came from thirteen years of enterprise workflows.

What this page can’t settle: which events a bank’s systems can promise, what a supplier’s receivables desk does with a notice, and where compliance draws the line.

An agent will build whichever record you ask for. Deciding which fact goes first is still the hard part.
