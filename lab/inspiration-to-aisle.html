From Inspiration to Aisle

How might shopping feel like one continuous experience across digital discovery, a physical store and a person who works there? One trip, followed from a photo at a friend’s house to a store aisle, as questions, a journey, a service blueprint and a working prototype.

Try the prototype

An independent concept, designed and built by me with Claude Code. Not client work, and not affiliated with or sponsored by any retailer: the store is made up and every count is sample data. Nothing here has been tested with shoppers yet, and the page says which assumptions to test first.

The trip starts over at the door

You are at Maya’s, and you like her lamp. You take a picture of it so you remember. At home that night you search, find four that look close, and read enough to narrow it to two.

Two days later you are standing in a store. The shelf has lamps on it, but which two were yours? The app on your phone knows what you looked at, and the aisle does not. When you find someone who works there, they start from “What are you looking for?”

Every part of that trip works. The phone finds things, the store has them, the person knows the back room. What breaks is between them: the context does not travel. Three things a shopper built up at home are gone by the time they reach the aisle:

  • The question. The photo, which is what every later screen is really answering.
  • How far they got. The shortlist, and the comparison they half-finished at the kitchen table.
  • What they were unsure of. Whether it is the right height, whether the store really has it, and which difference matters.

I think this is a service design problem before it is a screen design problem. The screens are fine. The seams are where the trip falls apart, and a seam is something you can only see by drawing the whole trip.

Five questions I would want answered before building for real

I have not interviewed shoppers or store staff for this, and I have not invented any findings. Each question below comes with the assumption I made so the prototype could exist. The assumptions are mine, and they are what the study at the end is for.

  1. What needs to carry across channels, and what should not?My assumption: the photo, the shortlist and the comparison travel; the account, the purchase history and the name do not. Less than the system knows, and only what the person was thinking with.
  2. When does the AI help, and when does it intrude?My assumption: it helps when it explains a match in terms a person can check, and it intrudes when it speaks first in the aisle. At home it leads; in the store it waits to be asked.
  3. How do people judge a substitute?My assumption: by the one attribute that made them want the original, and then by the one difference they can see on the shelf. A substitute that names its difference is weighed; one that hides it is distrusted.
  4. What makes a shelf count trustworthy enough to drive for?My assumption: its age, printed beside it every time, and a store that admits when it cannot vouch for the number. A count with no age reads as a promise.
  5. What happens when the technology is wrong?My assumption: the shelf wins. The app should say so plainly, offer the ways forward with their differences named, and put a person one press away rather than argue.

Two of these I am fairly sure of; the second and third I am not. Which of them is right decides how much of this app should exist in the store at all, and that is the first thing I would test.

The journey, with its seams marked

Six moments, from the friend’s living room to a conversation in the aisle. Under each: the question in the shopper’s head, where today’s trip rubs, and what one connected experience could do about it. The figure is drawn from the scenario rather than from observed shoppers, and it says so.

Read across the third row and the pattern is the same at every moment: a system that knows something and a person who cannot get at it. The fourth row is one idea said six ways. Keep what the person built, say how sure you are, and know when to stop.

A simplified service blueprint

The journey says what the shopper feels. The blueprint says what has to be true behind it: what they see, what an employee does, what runs in the back, and which system each step leans on. The failure points are a lane of their own, because they are where this idea either holds or does not.

This is conceptual. It is not a description of any retailer’s infrastructure, and I have not seen one. Every system named is a guess about what a service like this would need.

Two things stand out once it is drawn. The timestamp on a count does more work than any screen: without it, moment three cannot be honest. And the employee is in two lanes, not one: they are backstage at moment three, counting, and front of house at moment five. The same person, and the blueprint is what shows that the first job makes the second one possible.

The prototype: the moment the phone goes into the store

I built the seam the journey keeps pointing at: from the photo, through the matches and the shortlist, into the aisle, and out to a person. Seven screens at a phone’s width, with every lamp, count and employee written in the source as sample data. No image search and no inventory system are connected, and the page says so.

Try the prototype

The prototype page walks through five interface decisions. The short version: the context travels, every match explains itself, a no costs one press, a shelf count is a claim with an age, and the hand-off to a person is designed rather than left to happen.

Where the person stays in charge

The AI here does three jobs: it matches, it explains, and it suggests alternatives. Everything else is the shopper’s. The rules for keeping it that way are the ones Agent Review and Dispatch Cockpit already work from, and they transfer almost unchanged:

  • Explain why, in terms the person can check. “Same shade, different legs” can be held up against the photo. “87% match” cannot.
  • Say how sure you are, in words. “A stretch” teaches what the app can see. A count two days old is marked before anyone drives for it.
  • Let people compare instead of accepting. The side-by-side data table is the shopper’s tool, not the app’s verdict.
  • Make rejecting easy and reversible. “Not it” sits beside “Save”, and Undo sits on the line that reports it.
  • Know when a person is more useful than automation. A back room and a judgment call about a near match belong to Dana, and the app hands her the context and steps back.

The concession: a store is not an operations floor. Dispatchers have to check the system’s work; shoppers can walk away from it. So the bar for intrusion is lower here, and “it waits to be asked” matters more than any explanation does.

What I would test first, and what would prove it wrong

Nothing here has been run, so there are no results to report. This is the plan, starting with the assumption that would cost the most if it is wrong: that a person in an aisle wants to look at their phone at all. If they do not, the store half of this app should shrink to an aisle number and a hand-off, and the work was in getting them to the right shelf with the right two lamps in mind.

  1. The study

    Five people who recently shopped for something they first saw somewhere else. Each brings a photo of their own. Two tasks: narrow it to two at home, then find them in a store with the phone in hand. Half the sessions in a real store aisle, half with a mock shelf, one lamp missing in both.

  2. What I would watch

    Whether they open the reasons or take the top match. Whether they notice the old count before deciding to drive. In the aisle, whether they look at the phone or the shelf first, and when, if ever, they ask for a person. A one-to-seven ease rating on each task, and the time each took.

  3. Success, and failure

    Worth building further if most people use the carried context in the aisle without being told it is there, and if the marked count changes at least one decision. Wrong if the phone comes out only to check out, or if the hand-off reads as being pushed to staff rather than helped.

The evidence that would justify building more is small and specific: people reaching for the shortlist in the aisle, and a store employee saying the attached context saved a question. The evidence that would stop it is just as specific, and I would rather find it in five sessions than after a build.

Question
Can one trip feel continuous across a phone, a store and a person?
Hypothesis
Carry the context, say how sure, hand off on purpose.
Prototype
Seven screens on sample data, built in a few days with Claude Code.
Evaluation
Not yet. The five-person study above is the next step.
Decision
Not made. It waits on the first sessions.
Learning so far
Drawing the blueprint moved the design: the count’s timestamp, not any screen, is the load-bearing part.

What got easier, and what did not

Building this took a few days. Ten years ago the prototype alone would have been a sprint for a small team, and the blueprint would have been the deliverable. Now the blueprint is a morning and the prototype is an afternoon, and I can put both in front of someone the same week.

What did not get easier is deciding what to carry across, what to say when the system is not sure, and when to stop and find a person. Those decisions were as slow as they ever were, and they are most of what makes the trip feel like one experience rather than three good products in a row.

I think that is the shape of design work now, in stores as much as in enterprise software. The seams are where the judgment lives, and a prototype is the fastest way to find out whether you drew them in the right place.
