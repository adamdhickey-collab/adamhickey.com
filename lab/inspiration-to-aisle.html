From Inspiration to Aisle

How might one shopping trip feel continuous across a phone, a store, and a person who works there? One trip, from a photo of a friend’s lamp to a store aisle, as a service map and a working prototype.

Try the prototype

An independent concept, designed and built by me with Claude Code. No retailer is involved: the store is made up, every count is sample data, and nothing has been tested with shoppers yet.

The trip starts over at the door

You like the lamp at a friend’s house, so you take a picture. That night you find four that look close and narrow it to two.

Two days later you’re in the store. Which two were yours? Your phone knows, the aisle doesn’t, and the person who works there starts with “What are you looking for?”

Every part of that trip works. What breaks is between them: the context doesn’t travel. That makes it a service design problem before a screen design problem, and a seam only shows when you draw the whole trip.

One trip, from the shopper down to the systems

Six moments across. The top lanes are what the shopper meets; under the line is what has to be true for it to work. Switch between today and the connected version to see where the trip breaks, and pick a moment to follow it down.

Read the Today lane across and it’s one failure six times: a system knows something a person can’t get at. Drawing it moved the design twice. The timestamp on a shelf count does more work than any screen, because without it moment three can’t be honest. And the employee shows up twice, counting at moment three and helping at moment six. The first job is what makes the second possible.

Five assumptions I built on

I haven’t interviewed shoppers or staff, and I haven’t invented findings. Each question carries the assumption I made so the prototype could exist.

  1. What should carry across, and what shouldn’t?Mine: the photo, the shortlist, and the comparison. Never the account or the purchase history.
  2. When does the AI help, and when does it intrude?Mine: at home it leads; in the store it waits to be asked.
  3. How do people judge a substitute?Mine: by the one thing they wanted from the original, and the one difference they can see.
  4. What makes a shelf count worth driving for?Mine: its age, printed beside it every time.
  5. What happens when the technology is wrong?Mine: the shelf wins, and a person is one press away.

The prototype: the moment the phone goes into the store

Seven screens at a phone’s width, from the photo to a person in the aisle, on sample data. No image search or inventory system is connected. Its page walks through the five interface decisions.

Where the person stays in charge

The AI matches, explains, and suggests. Everything else is the shopper’s, on the rules Agent Review and Dispatch Cockpit already use:

  • Explain in terms a person can check. “Same shade, different legs,” not “87% match.”
  • Say how sure, in words. “A stretch,” and a count marked two days old.
  • Compare, don’t accept. The data table is the shopper’s tool, not a verdict.
  • Make saying no cheap. “Not it” sits beside “Save,” with Undo on the line.
  • Hand off on purpose. The back room is Dana’s, and she gets the context.

One difference: a dispatcher has to check the system’s work, and a shopper can walk away. So here, waiting to be asked matters more than any explanation.

What I’d test first, and what would prove it wrong

Nothing has been run. The riskiest assumption is that a person in an aisle wants to look at their phone at all. If they don’t, the store half shrinks to an aisle number and a hand-off.

The study: five people who recently shopped for something they first saw somewhere else, each with a photo of their own. Narrow it to two at home, then find them in a store, with one lamp missing.

  • Worth building if most people reach for the shortlist in the aisle without being told, and the marked count changes at least one decision.
  • Wrong if the phone only comes out to pay, or the hand-off feels like being pushed onto staff.

What got easier, and what didn’t

The blueprint took a morning and the prototype an afternoon. Ten years ago the prototype alone was a sprint for a small team.

What didn’t get easier is deciding what to carry across, what to say when the system isn’t sure, and when to find a person. The seams are where the judgment lives, and a prototype is the fastest way to find out whether you drew them in the right place.
