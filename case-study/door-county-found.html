Independent project · Travel brand and guide · 7 min read

A travel guide that feels like advice from someone who knows the place

Door County Found is a curated guide to a Wisconsin peninsula my family has known for decades. Fewer places, every one vouched for, in an identity that looks like it was found in a drawer at the cabin. I made all of it, and my sister keeps it current.

For
My family, and anyone they’d send there
My part
Everything: concept, identity, writing system, build
Editor
My sister, through a web form
Live
doorcountyfound.com, 58 places and counting

01 · The idea

The opposite of a complete guide

Decades of family time on the peninsula built up real knowledge of it, and not only the summer kind. Door County is a year-round place, and the family knew which bluff to walk in February and which pier to jump off in July.

That knowledge lived where it usually lives: in heads, in one long document, in “ask your aunt.” The big guide sites compete on completeness and list every restaurant whether or not anyone would send a friend there.

So the product bet is the opposite. Fewer places, every one vouched for, each opening on a “why we love it” written the way the family talks. Best perch you’ll ever have. Real goats on the roof.

A guide like that needs to look like it came from a person, not a platform. That is where the identity started.

02 · The identity

Cream paper, harbor navy, beacon gold

The reference was the ephemera a trip leaves behind: a motel keyfob, a postcard, a fifteen-cent stamp, a chart folded one too many times. A vintage travel poster’s confidence without the pastiche.

The mark is a lighthouse in a navy disc with gold beams, the county’s name in condensed capitals and “found” in a rust script underneath, the one word in the identity that is handwritten. It is built by hand from an approved brand board, not themed.

  • Cream paperEvery page’s ground, with a grain
  • Harbor navyHeadlines, the mark, the map’s water
  • Beacon goldThe beams, the rules, the badges
  • Keyfob rustThe script, the second headline color
  • Shoreline tealThe map’s shallows, the dog page

03 · The voice

Every place reads like a recommendation

The writing system is one paragraph per place, in the family’s voice, and a small set of fixed fields around it: the town, the category, the address, whether dogs can come, what the vegetarians eat.

That split is what keeps the personality from getting in the way of the usefulness. The paragraph is allowed to be opinionated. The fields are allowed to be boring, and a dog policy is allowed to say “we need to check.”

04 · Photography and trust

Invented pictures for the brand, real ones for the places

A guide runs on trust, so the art direction draws a hard line. The campaign art, the drawn map, the cut-out ephemera: invention belongs there, in the spirit of the peninsula.

Beside a real restaurant or trail, the photograph has to be the place. The brand rules say it in one line: generated imagery is never used as if it shows a real venue, dish, room, trail or view. The stand-ins for judging a layout sit behind a flag and can’t reach a build.

The polaroid frames do the work of holding the two apart. A photograph in a frame with a handwritten town name is a snapshot someone took; the drawn map it sits on is the brand.

05 · Browsing

Cards that balance personality and usefulness

The index is a row of cards, each with its own photograph, its town and category stamped above the name, and a row of badges. The count at the top counts itself.

Because every place is a record, the ways in come free: dog-friendly and vegetarian are real filters rather than tags someone remembers to add, and the dogs page is a query sorted into the places where the answer is plainly yes and the nine that come with fine print. The map is there too, as a supporting feature, every town carrying its own count.

Why no lodging

One omission on purpose

Lodging is where every competitor fights, and listing it would turn a personal guide into a directory. Leaving it out keeps the guide personal and keeps it small enough for one person to maintain.

06 · Built to be kept

Built for its editor, not its builder

The real user of the workflow is my sister, who isn’t a developer and shouldn’t have to become one. She signs into a web form, fills in the fields and hits save, and the change is live about two minutes later. The product ships with its own operating manual, down to how the photo cropping works.

Under it, boring technology chosen on purpose: Astro, statically built, every place a validated markdown record; the identity hand-built rather than themed; Cloudflare Pages for about $12 a year; Pages CMS as the form over the repository’s own content. I switched editing tools once, mid-build, when the first one wanted a server and a GitHub App, exactly the moving parts this site exists to avoid.

I designed it, wrote the system, drew the identity into code and shipped it alone, with AI writing most of the code to my spec. The part worth showing is that it keeps running without me.

07 · What it shows

An identity, a voice and a product that agree with each other

What I did

  • The concept: a curated guide in a family’s voice, with no lodging
  • The identity, from an approved brand board: mark, palette, type, the ephemera and the drawn map
  • The writing system and the fields around it, and the art-direction rule on photographs
  • The site, the filters, the map and the editor’s workflow, built and handed over

What this page doesn’t claim

  • No traffic or usage figures; the guide is for a family and the people they send
  • The brand board is an illustration of the identity; the venue photographs on the site are real
  • The swatches are sampled from the board, not read from a spec
