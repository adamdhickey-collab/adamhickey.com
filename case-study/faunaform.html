How I’m building Faunaform

Seven Minnesota animals, each drawn for one hour of one day, to be screen printed by hand in four flat inks. An image model draws, software I wrote with Claude Code makes every drawing printable, and every call about the art is mine. More than ten rounds in its first six days, most of them stopped on purpose. Nothing is printed yet; the first proof is next.

The problem

An image model can draw a striking wildlife print in a minute. It can’t tell you whether the print will print. At six inches a screen holds a line down to about a millimeter and a half and no thinner, four inks have to land on top of each other, and every color has to be an ink you actually own. It can’t tell you whether the bear is a bear either: a black bear isn’t a small grizzly, and the difference is a handful of marks.

So the studio has the same problem as software an agent helped build. The output has to survive real constraints, and someone has to answer for every call. The collection is one idea, One day of light: seven animals, each under the sun at its own hour of one day and its own month of one year, from the great horned owl before dawn in January to the snowshoe hare at dusk in December.

Who does what

Every step of the work says who does it. It started as a note to myself, and it became the most useful line on every page about the work, because it’s the honest answer to the question everyone asks about AI and art.

  • Me. The idea, the research, every choice and the final say. And the printing.
  • An AI image model, ChatGPT. It draws and redraws a picture from a written brief, in about a minute a take.
  • An AI assistant, Claude. It helps with the research, turns a direction into a brief, and writes the studio’s software.
  • Studio software. Ordinary code, not AI, so it does exactly the same thing every time. It fixes the colors, measures the shapes and makes the screens.

Two habits came over from Lucy Learns. Every brief is a file, never a paragraph pasted into a chat. Every drawing records where it came from: its round, its chosen take and its source. That makes the work repeatable, and it’s the answer when a fair asks how a piece was made.

One print, start to finish

The black bear at golden hour, drawn between 16 and 19 October 2026. Every picture of a print on this page is a drawing or a simulation; nothing has been pulled yet.

An animal, an hour, a season

Every print starts from something the animal really does here. Black bears in Minnesota are out by day as well as at dawn and dusk, and blueberries ripen in July and August. So the bear is in the berry patch in August, head down, at golden hour, with the sun low behind the birches.

Old prints, one idea each

Claude searched the open collections of the Met, the Art Institute of Chicago and the Cleveland Museum of Art for pictures that solved a problem like this one, mostly old Japanese prints and design books. Each went on the board for one idea: a pose, a shape, a way of placing the sun. The pictures never go to the image model. Their ideas go into words.

Getting the animal right

The difference between a black bear and a small grizzly is a handful of marks, so they go into the brief word for word: a straight face from forehead to nose, no shoulder hump, tall, round-tipped ears, a tan muzzle. So do the plants that grow where it feeds, in the month it feeds there: paper birch, lowbush blueberry, bunchberry, bracken fern. An early drawing put the bear among yellow flowers that bloom in June. It moved to the August berry patch.

The brief, then the drawing

The brief carries the field marks, the plants, the hour, and which of the four inks each thing is printed in. One finished print from the series goes along, to show the style. The bear’s opened: “An American black bear on all fours in a paper birch grove in August, side-on facing right, head lowered toward a patch of lowbush blueberries.”

The image model draws a brief in about a minute. For a new animal I ask for three takes and push one further. The bear began as a dark dome under a huge sun, when the series was still planned in cut wood. A day later, with the series now one day of light, it stood in a birch grove at golden hour.

Saying what’s wrong

Most of the work is here: looking hard and saying exactly what isn’t right. The first square bear came back with a soft haze in its sky and a row of small triangle trees on the far shore. Every ink in these prints is flat, so it went back with a correction, and the second take mended both and kept the rest. A picture goes round like this until there’s nothing left I’d change.

Making it printable

A picture that looks right on screen isn’t finished until every shape will print. This is where the software takes over, because it’s the part that has to come out the same every time.

Exact inks

The image model’s colors are close, never exact, and a screen can only print the inks it has. The software gives every pixel one of the four inks or the bare paper, measures how far each moved, and leaves every shape one flat color with a clean edge.

Will it print?

The first prints are six inches square, and at that size a shape thinner than about 1.5 mm won’t hold ink cleanly. The software measures every shape and marks the ones too thin in magenta and the ones about to vanish in cyan. They go back to the image model with one rule: widen, never remove. Nothing should be narrower than the bear’s eye. The bear took two passes, then I approved it.

Smooth curves, then four screens

The picture is still made of pixels, which turn to stairs up close. A tracing tool, Vectorizer.AI, redraws every edge as a smooth curve, so the films come out crisp. Then the software splits the print into its screens, lighter inks under darker with a 0.3 mm overlap and registration marks on every film. The bear’s four inks fit on two screens.

The rules all of this answers to are written down: the print standard, nineteen of them across five stages, from how many inks a print may have to how thin a gap can be. Every rule is marked provisional until the first proof. The same inputs always give the same outputs.

The model widens the search. Code holds the production truth.

Three decisions that shaped it

The medium came last

It began on 14 October as laser-cut wood: a tool read the loon drawing as 76 shapes, 27 veneer pieces and three levels. Wood veneer, matboard and a wood collage followed, each judged on simulated renders rather than on materials bought. By the 16th it was screen printing, because a screen gives the flat, bold shapes the idea needs at a cost a small studio can carry. Trying four media in three days cost nothing but compute.

One idea, held across seven pieces

Golden hour here lasts thirty-five to fifty minutes, and in it sunlit things turn gold while shadows, lit only by the blue sky, turn blue-violet. Every hour has its own version of that, so every print takes its four inks from its own sky. Printmakers have done this for a century: Hiroshi Yoshida printed the same sailing boats six times, morning to night, from the same blocks in new colors. These prints do it with seven animals. Every later round had to keep that idea or go, and more than ten rounds went.

Say what AI did, in plain words

“AI-assisted”, never “AI-generated”. No render shown as a finished piece. “Designed to be screen printed by hand” until a proof exists. Some art fairs here exclude AI-assisted work, so organizers get asked before any application. Disclosure is a design problem too: research suggests a label that says AI changes what people will pay, so the wording has to be honest and considered at the same time.

How I’ll check it

Three things, in order.

  • The first proof. The bear, on paper and then on a birch panel. Every rule in the standard is confirmed or rewritten against it.
  • Buyer research before a price. Two rounds, six to eight people and then eight to ten, before anything is for sale.
  • Fairs asked first. Organizers asked about AI-assisted work before any application.

What it has to do with product design

It’s the argument of this site, applied to art. A standard every piece is checked against. Colors and sizes held as tokens. A decision log, and an archive of the rounds that didn’t make it. An image model to widen the search, software to hold what must not drift, and a person deciding what ships.

That’s the infrastructure behind great design, at the scale of one studio and one bear.
