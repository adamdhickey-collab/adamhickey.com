How I’m building Faunaform

Seven Minnesota animals, each drawn for one hour of one day, to be screen printed by hand in four flat inks. An image model draws, software I wrote with Claude Code makes every drawing printable, and every call about the art is mine. Nothing is printed yet; the first proof is next.

The problem

An image model can draw a striking wildlife print in a minute. It can’t tell you whether the print will print, or whether the bear is a bear.

Software an agent helped build has the same problem: someone has to answer for every call.

The collection is one idea, One day of light: seven animals, each at its own hour of one day, from the owl before dawn to the hare at dusk.

Who does what

Every step of the work says who does it, which answers the question everyone asks about AI and art.

  • Me. The idea, the research, every choice and the final say. And the printing.
  • An AI image model, ChatGPT. It draws from a written brief, about a minute a take.
  • An AI assistant, Claude. It helps with research, turns my direction into briefs and writes the studio’s software.
  • Studio software. Ordinary code, not AI: it fixes the colors, measures the shapes and makes the screens, the same way every time.

Every drawing also records its round, its take and its source, a habit from Lucy Learns. That’s the answer when a fair asks how a piece was made.

One print, start to finish

The black bear at golden hour, drawn from 16 to 20 October 2026.

Every print starts from something the animal really does here. Black bears feed by day and blueberries ripen in August, so the bear is in the berry patch, head down.

Old prints, one idea each

Claude searched three museums’ open collections for old prints that solved a problem like this one. Each went on the board for one idea: a pose, a shape, a way to place the sun.

New work, on Cosmos

For contemporary work Claude searched Cosmos, a site for collecting images, the same way. Neither set goes to the image model; their ideas go into the brief, in words.

Getting the animal right

A black bear isn’t a small grizzly, so the brief spells out the marks: a straight face, no shoulder hump, tall round-tipped ears, a tan muzzle.

The brief, then the drawing

The bear’s brief opened: “An American black bear on all fours in a paper birch grove in August, side-on facing right, head lowered toward a patch of lowbush blueberries.”

I ask for three takes of a new animal and push one further. The bear began as a dark dome under a huge sun, planned in wood; a day later it stood among birches at golden hour.

Saying what’s wrong

Most of the work is saying exactly what’s wrong. The first square bear had a hazy sky and a row of tiny triangle trees, and a haze can’t print in flat ink. I named both, and take two corrected them.

The fifth leg

Looking hard still misses things. I approved this bear on 19 October. Count its legs.

The brief had said “on all fours,” and the model drew five anyway. Saying it again wasn’t going to fix that. Four moves did, and they’re how I fix a drawing now.

Claude found the photo and read up on how black bears walk: the hind feet land flat, and the front legs never meet in an A. The photo is Courtney Celley’s, for the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service, and in the public domain.

The measuring is what lets me trust an edit. Software compares each one with the drawing it came from, pixel by pixel. The paws pass changed 1.1% of the picture, all of it at the paws.

The model drew each take in about a minute. Seeing the fifth leg took me a day.

Making it printable

Here the software takes over, because this part has to come out the same every time.

Exact inks

The image model’s colors are close, never exact. The software sets every pixel to one of the four inks or the bare paper, so every shape is one flat color with a clean edge.

Snapped to the inks, the far bush came out a blotch of two. The drawing didn’t go back. Since 22 October the software cleans the bear before its films: each shape the model drew in one color prints whole, in one ink.

Will it print?

At six inches, a shape thinner than about 1.5 mm won’t hold ink. The software marks each one in magenta, and the drawing goes back with one rule: widen, never remove.

Smooth curves, then four screens

Up close, pixels turn to stairs, so Vectorizer.AI redraws every edge as a smooth curve. Then the software splits the print into its screens, with registration marks on every film.

The model widens the search. Code holds the production truth.

Three decisions that shaped it

The medium came last

It began on 14 October as laser-cut wood, then veneer, matboard and collage, each judged on simulated renders. By the 16th it was screen printing. Four media in three days cost nothing but compute.

One idea, held across seven pieces

Every print takes its four inks from its own hour’s sky: gold where the sun lands, blue-violet in the shadows. Every round had to keep that idea or go, and more than ten went.

Say what AI did, in plain words

“AI-assisted,” never “AI-generated,” and no render shown as finished. A label that says AI can change what people will pay, so the wording has to be honest and careful.

How I’ll check it

  • The first proof. The bear, on paper and then on birch, with every rule confirmed or rewritten against it.
  • Buyers before a price. Two rounds of six to ten people.
  • Fairs asked first. Some here exclude AI-assisted work.

What it has to do with product design

It’s the argument of this site, applied to art: a standard every piece is checked against, a decision log, an image model to widen the search, software to hold what must not drift, and a person deciding what ships.

That’s the infrastructure behind great design, at the scale of one studio and one bear.
