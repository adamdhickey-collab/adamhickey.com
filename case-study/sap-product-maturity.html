Overview

The problem

The company ran more than 40 SAP apps, built by different teams at different times. Someone in the field might open four before lunch and relearn each layout, and nobody measured whether any of it worked.

The solution

I built one design system and one accessibility standard for all 40+ apps, with design checkpoints in the release process. The apps started to look and work alike, and design quality finally had numbers the business could see.

My part

I led the UX work: the usability testing and day-in-the-life interviews, the audits, the style guide, the release checkpoints, and the training. Product owners decided what shipped, developers built the components, and SAP’s managers backed the standard.

My approach

1Establish the foundation

Jakob’s Law says people expect an app to work like the ones they use most. In the field, those were the company’s other apps. We reviewed them all, then built one style guide on SAP’s Fiori design language for everyone to work from.

Doing it in that order was the decision. A component library would have been quicker to show, but built before the review it would have been a fifth version of everything. Reviewing first meant the library started from what the teams already had.

3Make quality routine

Design quality is decided between the moment a request comes in and the day the app ships. So UX and accessibility checks went in at every step along the way, and the standards started showing up in every release.

Cross-functional workflow for app feedback

Human judgment

A document is inherited by whoever reads it. A check in the release is inherited by every team that ships.

It had a cost. A check can stop a release, so teams pushed back, and every exception had to be argued. I took that cost, because an exception argued at a checkpoint is a decision someone made and can explain.

Evidence: the production-defect row and how it was counted.

4Get teams to own it

The standard couldn’t depend on me being in the room. So the product health scorecard went to the teams, and each ran its own UX checks. That is most of why it kept working after I left.

The impact

Dimension
Outcome
Scalability
Brought 40+ applications under one design system, replacing hundreds of one-off UI components with a small, reusable library.
Velocity
The shared library cut the average time from design to development by about a third.
Quality
Standards and accessibility checks built into the process cut UI defects in production roughly in half, quarter over quarter.
Training
Clearer flows cut new-user training time in half.
Adoption
Changes we made from user data lifted NPS by roughly 20 points across several release cycles.
How I measured this

How each figure above was counted, over what period, and what it cannot tell you.

Design to development, about a third less time
From the team’s work tracker: the time from a design being marked ready to development starting on it, averaged per story. It compares the two quarters before the shared library with the two quarters after it was in use. Story size was not normalized, so this is a trend across many stories, not a saving on any single one.
UI defects in production, roughly half
Production defects that QA tagged as UI (layout, labels, inconsistent components), counted per quarter. The drop followed the accessibility and design checkpoints going into the release process. Releases also got smaller over the same stretch, which helped.
New-user training time, cut in half
The training team’s figure: hours of instructor-led onboarding for a new field user, before and after the redesigned flows.
NPS, up roughly 20 points
The in-app survey the product team already ran each release, compared across several release cycles. Responses per release were modest, so the change is read across releases rather than from any one of them.
Hundreds of one-offs, one small library
An inventory of the interface components across the 40+ apps at the start, set against the shared library at the end.
