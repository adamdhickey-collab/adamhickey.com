How I built Lucy Learns

A dog-training app for my own household, and three tries at one problem: following instructions with a leash in one hand and a dog who doesn’t follow a script.

Role
Everything: research, interaction, illustration, build
For
Two handlers, one dog, and the trainer whose program it follows
Used
One-handed in a hallway, or propped on a stand, with no signal needed
Status
In use. The next version, a teleprompter, isn’t built yet

  1. 01Research

  2. 02Design

The problem

We hired a professional trainer for our dog and got a stack of handouts: good instructions, written for someone sitting still.

Practice happens in a hallway, leash in one hand and treat pouch in the other, five minutes at a time. By evening, nobody remembers whether the barking is getting better or it was just a bad Tuesday.

So the app has a narrow job: turn the handouts into something you can run one-handed, and record enough that “is this working?” has an answer. It supports the trainer. It doesn’t replace one.

Three tries at the same screen

Every version of the session screen has had one job: keep the handler’s eyes and hands on the dog. The first two failed at it in the hallway, and each failure told me what to try next.

Try one: talk to the phone

Voice looked like one feature. It was two: the app reading steps aloud, and the app listening for commands.

The listening half failed, and the microphone wasn’t why. It was a misconception, and it was mine: I designed the commands for the handler and forgot who else was listening.

Say “next step” brightly at a phone and you’re using the bright voice her cues come in. Lucy is being taught that words in that voice are for her, and every one distracted her.

The AI I built it with didn’t flag that. Neither did I. A couple of real sessions did, and listening came out.

Try two: let a timer walk the steps

So a timer walked the steps instead, each one moving on after a set number of seconds.

Then Lucy went to a training boot camp, and we ran real sessions from the handouts she came home with.

The timer didn’t hold up. A dog doesn’t keep time: when something goes wrong, the session pauses, and the clock doesn’t.

Try three: follow the handler

The next version is a teleprompter: the script in type you can read from across the hallway, keeping its place by hearing you.

It isn’t built, and I don’t know yet whether it holds up. Listening comes back with a different job, and it still needs a microphone in a room with a dog in it.

Do the pictures earn their place?

Every level opens on a picture, and getting them consistent took a month. A training app needs the same dog in the same room on every screen, with only the behavior changing.

The boot camp handouts raised a harder question. Does someone running a session need a picture of every step? I don’t think so.

The next version keeps one picture at the start of each exercise, to set the scene, and then goes straight to the script.

When a picture has to say something, it gets a cold read. Eight scenes went shuffled to four AI models that knew nothing about the project, and the two they all misread were redrawn.

What held through every version

Progress in the trainer’s units, not mine

The obvious move was points, streaks, or a satisfying progress bar. I used the trainer’s levels instead, so the number on the program map is the number in her lesson report.

Streaks use the goal-gradient effect: they pull people toward the app’s goal. The goal that matters here is Lucy’s next level.

Logging happens during the session, not after it

One tap after each rep, and one more to rate her arousal, meaning how worked up she got, which saves the session. That’s the whole logging burden, on purpose: anything heavier doesn’t get used with a dog attached to your other arm.

Use is the check that takes features out

The AI didn’t decide what to build. Every decision here came from watching two people try to practice with a dog, in a hallway, five minutes at a time.

Anything I’m not sure of ships switched off, gets turned on in the hallway, and earns its place or goes. Voice commands went. The timer is next. A feature that survives that is one I’ve watched work.

Try it

This is the app itself, live, at the width of a phone. You’re Fabiola, partway through her trainer’s program with Lucy. One session takes about a minute:

  • Start on Today. The row from Sound to Goal is the trainer’s program, and Greet is where Fabiola is. Tap Start session, then Start practice.
  • Walk one rep. Each screen is one instruction. Tap Next step through all five, then Went well.
  • Finish. Tap Finish session, then tap how calm Lucy was. That one tap saves it.
  • Open Progress in the bar at the bottom. The week is there, with a summary ready to hand to the trainer.

It’s made to be used one-handed, with a leash in the other hand, so it works best on a phone. Everything it saves stays in your own browser.
