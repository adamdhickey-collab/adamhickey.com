Personal product · Illustration and interface · 8 min read

Dog training doesn’t happen at a desk.

It happens in a hallway, with a leash in one hand and a treat pouch in the other. Lucy Learns turns a trainer’s handouts into something friendly enough to glance at and clear enough to follow, made for my own household and tested on our own dog.

For
Our household, and Lucy, a black Lab who gets excited at the door
My part
Everything: illustration, interface, writing, build
Status
In use on our phones. The teleprompter is a concept
Built as
An installable phone app that works offline, no framework

01 · The problem

Good instructions, written for someone sitting still

We hired a professional trainer for our dog and came home with a stack of handouts. They were good. They were also written for a person at a table, and practice happens in a hallway, five minutes at a time, with a dog who doesn’t follow a script.

By evening nobody remembers whether the barking is getting better or it was just a bad Tuesday.

So the app has a narrow job: turn the handouts into something you can run with your eyes on the dog, and record enough that “is this working?” has an answer. It supports the trainer. It doesn’t replace one.

That job set the design question for everything after it. How do you make an instruction friendly and clear when the reader can only glance at it?

02 · The illustration language

The same dog, in the same room, on every screen

A training app could look like a fitness app: bold type, progress rings, a lot of red. I wanted it to look like our house. Every level opens on a drawing of the handler and Lucy in a room you recognize, with only the behavior changing from scene to scene.

Getting that consistency took a month and four passes. The first set was painted and full of detail that vanished at the 56 pixels the program map gives a scene. The last is flat, one action a frame, on a warm plaster wall, and it holds up at any size.

How the pictures are made

A spec per scene, not a chat window

Each scene is a short written spec that a script sends to an image model with the reference pictures in a set order, and every round is on file. The drawing is generated; the direction, the rules and every accept-or-redraw call are mine. A chat window can make one picture. It cannot repaint sixty-three walls between lunch and dinner.

03 · Understood at a glance

One instruction a screen, readable from a step away

The session screen carries one thing at a time, set large enough to read from arm’s length or from a table across the room. One tap per rep, one more on arousal to save, and that is the whole logging burden on purpose.

Every part of the screen does one job for a person who can only glance. The illustration says which scene you are in before you read a word. The instruction sits alone, in large type, above the controls, the pause, the next step’s timer, the rep tally and a miss, so nothing competes with it for the glance and a thumb finds the same places every time.

Progress is in the trainer’s units. The obvious move was points and streaks; I used her levels instead, so the number on the program map is the number in her lesson report. Points would have looked more like a product and been worse at the job.

04 · Watching the real session

Lucy heard every command as hers

The first version let you talk to the phone. Say “next step” and it moved on, which looked like the perfect answer to a handler with no free hand.

Then we ran a real session. Say “next step” brightly at a phone and you’re using the cue voice, the one Lucy is being taught means words for her. She was distracted by every one of them. The microphone worked fine. The misconception was mine: I designed the commands for the handler and forgot who else was listening.

Listening came out after a couple of sessions. A timer walked the steps instead, which held until Lucy’s boot camp handouts showed that a dog doesn’t keep time: when something goes wrong the session pauses and the clock doesn’t. The next version is a teleprompter that keeps its place by hearing the handler, and it is a concept, not a screen.

What the tries have in common is that the hallway found every problem, and each fix changed the approach rather than polishing the one before.

05 · Two postures

In one hand, or on a stand across the room

The session screen is designed around where your hands are, which turns out to be two different places. Phone in whichever hand the leash leaves free, or the phone or an iPad propped up where you practice, read from a step away with both hands on the dog. Nothing about the screen changes between them.

06 · What it shows

Use is the check that takes features out

No decision here came from a spec. Every one came from watching two people try to practice with a dog, in a hallway, five minutes at a time. Voice commands went. The timer is next. A feature that survives that is one I’ve watched work.

The technology is plain on purpose: JavaScript modules, a service worker for offline, local storage for state, the browser’s own speech synthesis to read the steps aloud. No framework, no build step, no account. An AI built working versions in an afternoon; I judged them.

What I did

  • The illustration language, its rules and the specs that drew sixty-three scenes and twenty-four portraits
  • The interface: one instruction a screen, logging during the session, progress in the trainer’s levels
  • The session screen three times, and the decision to remove listening after watching Lucy react to it
  • The build, as an installable offline app, with AI writing code to my direction

What this page doesn’t claim

  • No user research or usability study: one household, one dog, real sessions
  • The teleprompter is a mockup and the glasses are an idea; neither is built
  • The four-model reading check tests whether a drawing reads, not whether people like the app

Try it

This is the app itself, live, at the width of a phone. Run a session; everything it saves stays in your own browser, and nothing reaches Lucy’s phone. It is built for a hand and a leash, so it is better still on yours.
